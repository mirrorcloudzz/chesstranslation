Introduction  
<!-- AA-p006-l002 -->
Action is the essential basis on which the game of chess is founded and any ac-  
<!-- AA-p006-l003 -->
tion which contains a threat - i.e. attack in its widest sense -- stands out as a prom-  
<!-- AA-p006-l004 -->
inent feature of the game. To the outside observer, a chess game is dominated by  
<!-- AA-p006-l005 -->
the conflict between the two players. The ultimate aim of each player is, as a rule,  
<!-- AA-p006-l006 -->
the mating of the opponent's king, and an action with this aim, whether it is direct  
<!-- AA-p006-l007 -->
or indirect, is called an attack (in a narrower sense of the word) or a mating at-  
<!-- AA-p006-l008 -->
tack; that is the subject of this book.  
<!-- AA-p006-l009 -->
Being the most important action in chess and the central element of the game  
<!-- AA-p006-l010 -->
in ancient times, attack appears at all stages of the game's development and in  
<!-- AA-p006-l011 -->
various forms of perfection. Moreover, it can be said that the reforms which have  
<!-- AA-p006-l012 -->
been made in the rules of chess have always been in the direction of increasing  
<!-- AA-p006-l013 -->
and stimulating the opportunities for attack.  
<!-- AA-p006-l014 -->
The great reform of c. 1485, which created modern European chess, was par-  
<!-- AA-p006-l015 -->
ticularly responsible for opening up new opportunities for attack and ushered in a  
<!-- AA-p006-l016 -->
period of rich development in chess technique. For three centuries of chess his-  
<!-- AA-p006-l017 -->
tory, attack predominated over defence in the practice of the great players, and  
<!-- AA-p006-l018 -->
mastery at that time meant skill in conducting an attack. Only with Philidor did  
<!-- AA-p006-l019 -->
the first positional ideas appear, and with them more mature defensive strategies;  
<!-- AA-p006-l020 -->
these were to find in Steinitz a century later a legislator of genius.  
<!-- AA-p006-l021 -->
During the classic era of chess from Morphy to Steinitz and on to Lasker the  
<!-- AA-p006-l022 -->
value placed on attack gradually decreased, for with greater positional under-  
<!-- AA-p006-l023 -->

<!-- AA-p006-l024 -->
technique. This, however, was followed by a new period in which Capablanca,  
<!-- AA-p006-l025 -->
and particularly Alekhine, perfected the technique of attack, above all that of the  
<!-- AA-p006-l026 -->
attack on the castled position, founded on exact positional play. With Alekhine  
<!-- AA-p006-l027 -->
the aggressive and dynamic style of play reached a zenith; in the period which  
<!-- AA-p006-l028 -->
followed the tide again turned away gradually from the risks of the direct attack  
<!-- AA-p006-l029 -->
in search of new paths. The main reason for this is not to be found in any weak-  
<!-- AA-p006-l030 -->
ness inherent in the attacking style, but in the simple fact that, given the condi-

tions of present-day tournaments, it is more profitable and advantageous to make  
<!-- AA-p006-l032 -->
a study of openings. Now, it must be understood that the present theory of open-  
<!-- AA-p006-l033 -->
ings represents a detailed development of Nimzowitsch's ideas concerning the  
<!-- AA-p006-l034 -->
central squares. There are still many gaps to be filled and there is a wealth of op-  
<!-- AA-p006-l035 -->
portunity for innovation; as a result, great masters are inclined to concern

The basic pattern of mate  
<!-- AA-p009-l011 -->
For mate to be obtained, the king must be deprived of nine squares if it is in the  
<!-- AA-p009-l012 -->
middle of the board, six squares if it is on the edge, or four if it is in the corner.  
<!-- AA-p009-l013 -->
Some of these squares may be blocked by the king's own supporting pieces, but  
<!-- AA-p009-l014 -->
the rest must be taken by the attacker through the agency of his own pieces or  
<!-- AA-p009-l015 -->
pawns. If all the squares surrounding the king are taken from it -- some through  
<!-- AA-p009-l016 -->
being blocked, others by being ‘in check' - and if the square on which the king  
<!-- AA-p009-l017 -->
stands is also in check and without means of defence, then the king is check-  
<!-- AA-p009-l018 -->
mated. This is well known, and it seems rather banal to start out from this basic  
<!-- AA-p009-l019 -->
pattern or anatomy of mate; but in fact certain elementary rules can be formu-  
<!-- AA-p009-l020 -->
lated only on this basis.

The final or mating position is called the mating pattern, and this term is espe-  
<!-- AA-p010-l004 -->
cially useful when such a pattern is worth remembering. A mating pattern can be  
<!-- AA-p010-l005 -->
typical (i.e. one which frequently occurs) or atypical. We call a mate (or mating  
<!-- AA-p010-l006 -->
pattern) pure if none of the squares concerned is covered by two or more attack-  
<!-- AA-p010-l007 -->
ing units. Thus 1...e2 2 We6 is a pure mate, whereas 1...f6 2 &g7 is not,  
<!-- AA-p010-l008 -->
though it would be if we were to remove the bishop on f5. Pure mates are valued  
<!-- AA-p010-l009 -->
in chess problems, but in practical play they are unimportant.  
<!-- AA-p010-l010 -->
In the mating patterns which we have seen, some of the black king's flight  
<!-- AA-p010-l011 -->
squares have been blocked by its own pieces, while the rest have been covered by  
<!-- AA-p010-l012 -->
the opponent's forces. The sum of these combined factors is known as a mating  
<!-- AA-p010-l013 -->
net.

Mating square and focal-point  
<!-- AA-p010-l015 -->
The mating square is that on which the king stands when it is mated, and the mat-  
<!-- AA-p010-l016 -->
ing focal-point is that from which an opponent's piece (other than a king, knight  
<!-- AA-p010-l017 -->
or pawn) mates the king at close quarters. Thus in the above diagram, after  
<!-- AA-p010-l018 -->
1...e2 the focal-point is e6, since it is from there that the queen mates at close  
<!-- AA-p010-l019 -->
quarters. Similarly, after 1...gxf5 2 &g7+ e4 the queen mates at close quarters  
<!-- AA-p010-l020 -->
from d4, and that square is then the focal-point. However, if Black plays, for in-  
<!-- AA-p010-l021 -->

<!-- AA-p010-l022 -->

<!-- AA-p010-l025 -->

<!-- AA-p010-l026 -->

Where does the risk attached to a mating attack lie?  
<!-- AA-p012-l004 -->
About which points are the minds of players today still unclear with respect to  
<!-- AA-p012-l005 -->
carrying out a mating attack?  
<!-- AA-p012-l006 -->
The relationship between these questions can be easily perceived, although  
<!-- AA-p012-l007 -->
they are posed separately. A separate reply, however, cannot be given to each,  
<!-- AA-p012-l008 -->
and an attempt must be made to answer them collectively.  
<!-- AA-p012-l009 -->
Mate is the final aim and crowning achievement of the contest and the mating  
<!-- AA-p012-l010 -->
attack is the ultimate operation in relation to all the rest, which are only contribu-  
<!-- AA-p012-l011 -->
tory. Consequently, the mating attack demands more preconditions than other  
<!-- AA-p012-l012 -->
operations. There exist a number of definite preconditions for a mating attack,  
<!-- AA-p012-l013 -->
and these combine to require a considerable degree of superiority on the part of  
<!-- AA-p012-l014 -->
the attacker. In the case where he has abundant superiority, great knowledge and  
<!-- AA-p012-l015 -->
ability on the part of the attacker are not necessary, but if his superiority just bor-  
<!-- AA-p012-l016 -->
ders on what would justify a correct attack, then the maximum skill is also re-  
<!-- AA-p012-l017 -->
quired in carrying out the attack. The Romantics knew little, but they had great  
<!-- AA-p012-l018 -->
confidence in themselves; the first small increase in knowledge diverted their  
<!-- AA-p012-l019 -->
style from its course. The new formula, still accepted today, is: carry out a series  
<!-- AA-p012-l020 -->
of preliminary operations, which require only a smaller degree of advantage, and  
<!-- AA-p012-l021 -->
then you will attain the abundant superiority which facilitates a mating attack.  
<!-- AA-p012-l022 -->
However the consequence of this is that there has been a failure to exploit the art  
<!-- AA-p012-l023 -->
of attack to the full and masters have been over-inclined to see a risk in an attack.  
<!-- AA-p012-l024 -->
It is still not clear what the minimum preconditions are, or what kind of advan-  
<!-- AA-p012-l025 -->
tage is necessary, for a mating attack. It may be a question, for example, of a sin-  
<!-- AA-p012-l026 -->
gle moment in a game which never returns. Another still unresolved question is  
<!-- AA-p012-l027 -->
that of a player's skill in weighing up the obligations which he has undertaken  
<!-- AA-p012-l028 -->
when preparing for an attack. The order of his preparatory moves is extraordi-  
<!-- AA-p012-l029 -->
narily important. The only really simple thing is the principle itself - that moves  
<!-- AA-p012-l030 -->
entailing fewer obligations should be carried out before those which are more  
<!-- AA-p012-l031 -->
strongly binding. Putting this principle into practice, however, demands great  
<!-- AA-p012-l032 -->
skill of a kind which is very rare - it was fully possessed by Capablanca and  
<!-- AA-p012-l033 -->
Alekhine, but by few others.  
<!-- AA-p012-l034 -->
A few more words should be said about the kind of superiority which is neces-  
<!-- AA-p012-l035 -->
sary before a mating attack can be undertaken. We are interested in the case  
<!-- AA-p012-l036 -->
where the advantage is not one of material but of position and time. The attacker  
<!-- AA-p012-l037 -->
is, as a rule, stronger in the area where the attack takes place by virtue of his ma-  
<!-- AA-p012-l038 -->
terial preponderance there, but his superiority in this respect must be consider-  
<!-- AA-p012-l039 -->
able. Even a simple pattern with the queen mating from the focal-point needs at  
<!-- AA-p012-l040 -->
least one other piece to cover the focal-point, which already means an advantage  
<!-- AA-p012-l041 -->
of two pieces against a lone king. For such pieces to be brought into the attack,

<!-- AA-p013-l023 -->

<!-- AA-p013-l025 -->

<!-- AA-p013-l026 -->
Out, since otherwise one's advantage will evaporate.
