on the castled king  
<!-- AA-p247-l004 -->
In presenting, up to here, the various aspects of the attack on the castled king we  
<!-- AA-p247-l005 -->
have looked at the operation from the attacker's point of view. However, it is also  
<!-- AA-p247-l006 -->
true that the defence against such an attack has its own principles and particular  
<!-- AA-p247-l007 -->
characteristics, and it is therefore fitting that we should also try to discuss this de-  
<!-- AA-p247-l008 -->
fence systematically.  
<!-- AA-p247-l009 -->
Chess literature tends for the most part to talk of active and passive defence as  
<!-- AA-p247-l010 -->
being the two main types of defence. Steinitz also introduced the term 'self-  
<!-- AA-p247-l011 -->
defence', and H. Kmoch (in his work The Art of Defence) speaks of 'automatic  
<!-- AA-p247-l012 -->
defence'. Although it is now fairly well established, this division is not really the  
<!-- AA-p247-l013 -->
happiest one for teaching purposes; moreover, when it is a question of defence  
<!-- AA-p247-l014 -->
against an attack on the castled king, it is an imprecise one.  
<!-- AA-p247-l015 -->
I think it better to talk of direct and indirect defence of the castled position, in  
<!-- AA-p247-l016 -->
which case the direct defence is predominantly 'passive' and the indirect always  
<!-- AA-p247-l017 -->
'active'. The difference between these kinds of defence is usually clearly  
<!-- AA-p247-l018 -->
marked, since the former involves the defence of the actual castling area or the  
<!-- AA-p247-l019 -->
removal of one's opponent's aggressive pieces, while in the latter case it is a  
<!-- AA-p247-l020 -->
question of actions further away from the castled position, which the defender  
<!-- AA-p247-l021 -->
uses to keep the attacker so busy that he fails to pursue his attack to its conclu-  
<!-- AA-p247-l022 -->
Sion.  
<!-- AA-p247-l023 -->
In actual play direct defensive moves usually occur in conjunction with those  
<!-- AA-p247-l024 -->
of an indirect kind, and the main rule is that direct defence should be economical  
<!-- AA-p247-l025 -->
and that indirect should be executed at the right moment. Economy in direct de-  
<!-- AA-p247-l026 -->
fence consists of not making superfluous moves and not employing an unneces-  
<!-- AA-p247-l027 -->
sarily large number of pieces in defence. The right moment for indirect defence  
<!-- AA-p247-l028 -->
is fairly difficult to gauge, since it is normally determined by two interacting fac-  
<!-- AA-p247-l029 -->
tors; on the one hand, when direct defence has reached the limits that necessary  
<!-- AA-p247-l030 -->
economy allows, and on the other, when the attacker has really made a move  
<!-- AA-p247-l031 -->
which commits him fairly strongly, i.e. he has staked his money on the success of  
<!-- AA-p247-l032 -->
the attack.  
<!-- AA-p247-l033 -->
First, we shall set out clearly the various elements which go to make up each  
<!-- AA-p247-l034 -->
type of defence.

A. Direct Defence  
<!-- AA-p248-l004 -->
1) Protecting the squares of the castled position, including so-called over-  
<!-- AA-p248-l005 -->
protection.  
<!-- AA-p248-l006 -->
2) Transforming the castled position by moving the pawns in front of the  
<!-- AA-p248-l007 -->
king, a particular case being the setting-up of a blocked position.  
<!-- AA-p248-l008 -->
3) Altering the castled position by moving the king. Here it should be kept in  
<!-- AA-p248-l009 -->
mind that each move by the king changes the situation, changes the conditions  
<!-- AA-p248-l010 -->
necessary for an attack and, above all, changes the possible focal-points. King  
<!-- AA-p248-l011 -->
moves fall into the following graded categories:  
<!-- AA-p248-l012 -->
a) The consolidating move (e.g. Ébl in the case of queenside castling and  
<!-- AA-p248-l013 -->
in the case of kingside castling);  
<!-- AA-p248-l014 -->
b) A more radical alteration of the situation as a result of moving the king  
<!-- AA-p248-l015 -->
(e.g. to n, thus producing a so-called 'semi-castled' position);  
<!-- AA-p248-l016 -->
c) A king's flight, i.e. when it goes out in front of the castled position. Steinitz  
<!-- AA-p248-l017 -->
called this the king's self-defence, an older and more correct term than that of  
<!-- AA-p248-l018 -->
'automatic defence' applied by Kmoch.  
<!-- AA-p248-l019 -->
4) Defence by means of repulsing one's opponent's pieces, through their cap-  
<!-- AA-p248-l020 -->
ture, exchange, or ejection from powerful positions.  
<!-- AA-p248-l021 -->
B. Indirect Defence  
<!-- AA-p248-l022 -->
Before considering the ways of using the indirect method we must first of all ex-  
<!-- AA-p248-l023 -->
amine three aspects of such a defence. The first is purely spatial, i.e. whether the  
<!-- AA-p248-l024 -->
defender carries out his own counterattack in the centre or on the wings; the sec-  
<!-- AA-p248-l025 -->
ond is concerned with the degree of the threat; and the third with the question of  
<!-- AA-p248-l026 -->
whether the indirect action turns at some point into the direct method of defence.  
<!-- AA-p248-l027 -->
In the following review we shall combine these three aspects:  
<!-- AA-p248-l028 -->
l) The defender carnes out a counterattack on the opposite side, where the at-  
<!-- AA-p248-l029 -->
tacker has castled. In such a case his action is usually itself an attack on the cas-  
<!-- AA-p248-l030 -->
tled king, the position being one where the players have castled on opposite  
<!-- AA-p248-l031 -->
sides.  
<!-- AA-p248-l032 -->
2) An action on the opposite side first of all prepares the ground (files, ranks,  
<!-- AA-p248-l033 -->
etc.), and then turns into an outflanking manoeuvre against the attacker's castled  
<!-- AA-p248-l034 -->
position, i.e. while the attacker is attacking along the files, the defender gains  
<!-- AA-p248-l035 -->
control of the attacker's first or second rank.  
<!-- AA-p248-l036 -->
In this case, as in '1', the critical question is naturally either who will be the  
<!-- AA-p248-l037 -->
first to force mate or which of the two attacks will be brought to a halt through the  
<!-- AA-p248-l038 -->
diversion of pieces to provide a direct defence.

<!-- AA-p249-l003 -->
3) An action on any part of the board with the aim of gaining material.  
<!-- AA-p249-l004 -->
Such an action obviously only has a point when a forced mate is not part of the  
<!-- AA-p249-l005 -->
attacker's plan. The critical continuation then is that which costs the defender the  
<!-- AA-p249-l006 -->
least. For instance, if he can 'pacify the attacker's wrath' at the price of a knight,  
<!-- AA-p249-l007 -->
then a counterattack which brings him a rook is worthwhile.  
<!-- AA-p249-l008 -->
In practice the most important case is where the attacker sacrifices; the de-  
<!-- AA-p249-l009 -->
fender's repertoire is then greatly increased, since he is given various ways in  
<!-- AA-p249-l010 -->
which to return material in order to counter the attacker's plan. This method of  
<!-- AA-p249-l011 -->
returning material is extremely important; the extent to which it is possible af-  
<!-- AA-p249-l012 -->
fects the type of sacrifice which can be made, and the sacrifice is also a signal to  
<!-- AA-p249-l013 -->
the defender to counter with a sacrifice which would previously have been out of  
<!-- AA-p249-l014 -->
the question.  
<!-- AA-p249-l015 -->
4) The counterattack results in material equality, but the question of position  
<!-- AA-p249-l016 -->
still remains open. Mate cannot be forced, both attacks are brought to a halt, and  
<!-- AA-p249-l017 -->
eventual simplification leads towards an ending. The resulting position, which is  
<!-- AA-p249-l018 -->
often extremely difficult to assess beforehand, is then decisive.  
<!-- AA-p249-l019 -->
5) The defender's counterattack does not lead to a mating attack, nor to mate-  
<!-- AA-p249-l020 -->
rial or positional advantages, but instead consists of an eventual transition to the  
<!-- AA-p249-l021 -->
direct method.  
<!-- AA-p249-l022 -->
As a rule, this takes the form of either attacking the enemy pieces 'from the  
<!-- AA-p249-l023 -->
rear' or of gaining control of a file or diagonal so as to cover a certain square in  
<!-- AA-p249-l024 -->
one's king position (e.g. when the white queen has captured the square c7 and  
<!-- AA-p249-l025 -->
from there covers the focal-point h2).  
<!-- AA-p249-l026 -->
6) A special case of the kind of counterattack mentioned in '3' is provided by  
<!-- AA-p249-l027 -->
the advance of a queenside pawn majority. Its aim is to promote a pawn to a  
<!-- AA-p249-l028 -->
queen, and such an advantage justifies the most generous surrender of material in  
<!-- AA-p249-l029 -->
order to curb the attacker's forces.  
<!-- AA-p249-l030 -->
7) Retaliation in the centre can have the features of sections '3' to '5' either  
<!-- AA-p249-l031 -->
separately or combined. The following possibilities are particularly worth noting  
<!-- AA-p249-l032 -->
here:  
<!-- AA-p249-l033 -->
a) A central thrust demolishes or weakens the attacker's formation there and  
<!-- AA-p249-l034 -->
prepares the ground for further operations by the defender, or it enables him to  
<!-- AA-p249-l035 -->
transpose to direct defence 'via a turning manoeuvre' ;  
<!-- AA-p249-l036 -->
b) The destruction of the pawn centre eliminates its auxiliary function with  
<!-- AA-p249-l037 -->
regard to an attack on the castled king. For example, the disappearance of a pawn  
<!-- AA-p249-l038 -->
on e5 can also mean the end of an attack on the castled king, if the pawn is vital to  
<!-- AA-p249-l039 -->
a certain continuation;  
<!-- AA-p249-l040 -->
c) Because of the danger of a central thrust, many attackers at a suitable mo-  
<!-- AA-p249-l041 -->
ment deliberately create a blocked position in the centre. As a rule such a central

<!-- AA-p250-l004 -->

This outline of defensive methods brings out above all else the richness of

<!-- AA-p250-l017 -->

<!-- AA-p250-l019 -->

<!-- AA-p250-l020 -->

<!-- AA-p250-l021 -->
place in the defender's repertoire, and so every attacker must be able to calculate  
<!-- AA-p250-l022 -->
accurately what is likely to happen in the centre during an attack on the castled  
<!-- AA-p250-l023 -->

<!-- AA-p283-l006 -->

<!-- AA-p283-l007 -->

<!-- AA-p283-l012 -->

<!-- AA-p283-l014 -->

<!-- AA-p283-l015 -->
