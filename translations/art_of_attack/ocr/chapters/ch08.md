The queen in the attack on the castled king  
<!-- AA-p183-l010 -->
The queen is undoubtedly the most important piece in the attack on the castled  
<!-- AA-p183-l011 -->
king, and indeed without it such an attack rarely comes into consideration. Ad-  
<!-- AA-p183-l012 -->
mittedly, it is not impossible to undertake a direct attack without the queen, but  
<!-- AA-p183-l013 -->
the necessary conditions for it cannot easily be created.  
<!-- AA-p183-l014 -->
The queen's great mobility, its main characteristic, is a useful factor in the at-  
<!-- AA-p183-l015 -->
tack on the castled king, but its effectiveness at short range is even more striking.  
<!-- AA-p183-l016 -->
If it is in occupation of the focal-point g7, the enemy king on g8 has every square  
<!-- AA-p183-l017 -->
taken from it; moreover, if it is protected on that focal-point and the focal-point  
<!-- AA-p183-l018 -->
itself has been cleared of the influence of the opposing pieces, then the king is  
<!-- AA-p183-l019 -->
mated. The queen is, as it were, 'made for focal-points', and particularly for play  
<!-- AA-p183-l020 -->
on compound focal-points. Therefore, the queen's chief strategy in an attack on  
<!-- AA-p183-l021 -->
the castled king consists in playing on the focal-points and discovering ways in  
<!-- AA-p183-l022 -->
which to attack as effectively as possible at close range. Thus the queen, which in  
<!-- AA-p183-l023 -->
an endgame on an open board feels such an aversion towards the opponent's king  
<!-- AA-p183-l024 -->
that it will not allow it nearer than two paces away, is filled in attack with some  
<!-- AA-p183-l025 -->
kind of dark desire and dreams only of how it can steal into the castled dwelling  
<!-- AA-p183-l026 -->
and there fold the king in a close and deadly embrace.  
<!-- AA-p183-l027 -->

<!-- AA-p183-l028 -->

The heavy pieces in the attack  
<!-- AA-p186-l009 -->
The examples in chapters 4-7 have shown us various kinds of operations on the  
<!-- AA-p186-l010 -->
part of the rook, the bishop, and the knight on lines and focal-points, and no fur-  
<!-- AA-p186-l011 -->

<!-- AA-p186-l012 -->

<!-- AA-p186-l013 -->

<!-- AA-p186-l014 -->

The sacrifice in the attack on the castled king  
<!-- AA-p190-l008 -->
Before we turn to the role of the pawns in the attack on the castled king, a few  
<!-- AA-p190-l009 -->
general remarks must be made abont sacrifices in the castling area. Certain  
<!-- AA-p190-l010 -->
combinative elements are present in the case of every sacrifice, though these sac-  
<!-- AA-p190-l011 -->
rifices may be of widely diverging types. However, the typical sacrifice in the  
<!-- AA-p190-l012 -->
castling area is the one which is closely influenced by the special characteristics  
<!-- AA-p190-l013 -->
of that area, namely the castled king and the pawns in front of it. The typical sac-  
<!-- AA-p190-l014 -->
rifice either changes the structure of the pawns, or aims to draw the king on to a  
<!-- AA-p190-l015 -->
square where it can be attacked, or else helps to create a focal-point. We have al-  
<!-- AA-p190-l016 -->
ready met examples of the last two types in the course of discussing focal-points,  
<!-- AA-p190-l017 -->
particularly in the case of the classic bishop sacrifice. Here our principal interest  
<!-- AA-p190-l018 -->
is the pawn structure and the possibility of altering it by means of a sacrifice. A  
<!-- AA-p190-l019 -->
sacrifice can either simply annihilate one of the pawns in front of the king, or it  
<!-- AA-p190-l020 -->
can deflect the pawn on to another file, or finally it can cause it to be blocked.  
<!-- AA-p190-l021 -->
The main use of the sacrifice in the attack on the castled king is to eliminate  
<!-- AA-p190-l022 -->
the pawns in the castling area, and a number of examples will be devoted to this.  
<!-- AA-p190-l023 -->

The pawn avalanche  
<!-- AA-p213-l004 -->
The formation composed of two or three neighbouring pawns on the same rank is  
<!-- AA-p213-l005 -->
often called a 'phalanx ' in chess literature, but when such a phalanx attacks it can  
<!-- AA-p213-l006 -->
also be suitably described as an 'avalanche' or 'roller'. An attack based on a  
<!-- AA-p213-l007 -->
pawn avalanche generally entails even more positional commitments than the  
<!-- AA-p213-l008 -->

<!-- AA-p213-l009 -->

<!-- AA-p213-l010 -->

<!-- AA-p213-l011 -->

<!-- AA-p213-l012 -->

<!-- AA-p213-l013 -->

<!-- AA-p213-l014 -->

<!-- AA-p213-l016 -->
more restricted positionally and the actual process of advancing pawns is slower

<!-- AA-p213-l018 -->
