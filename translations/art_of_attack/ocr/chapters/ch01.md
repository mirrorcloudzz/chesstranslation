1 The attack against the  
<!-- AA-p014-l002 -->
uncastled king  
<!-- AA-p014-l003 -->
Making a mating attack on a king which had not yet castled was one of the joys of  
<!-- AA-p014-l004 -->
chess players in the past. These days the victim of such an attack is usually some-  
<!-- AA-p014-l005 -->
one playing a master in a simultaneous display who has failed to castle at the  
<!-- AA-p014-l006 -->
right time.  
<!-- AA-p014-l007 -->
The initial position of the king before it castles contains two main weaknesses.  
<!-- AA-p014-l008 -->
One is that it is exposed if the e-file is opened up; the second is that the square f7  
<!-- AA-p014-l009 -->
in Black's position (f2 is the corresponding weak point for White) is vulnerable,  
<!-- AA-p014-l010 -->
since it is covered by the king alone. It is therefore natural that the vast majority  
<!-- AA-p014-l011 -->
of attacks on an uncastled king exploit one of these weaknesses.  
<!-- AA-p014-l012 -->
The attack along the e-file  
<!-- AA-p014-l013 -->
The first and most fundamental condition for an attack along the e-file is that the  
<!-- AA-p014-l014 -->
opponent's king should be on that file, and that for some reason it is impossible  
<!-- AA-p014-l015 -->
or difficult for it to move away. If all the adjacent squares are occupied by the  
<!-- AA-p014-l016 -->
king's own pieces or controlled by the opponent's, its escape is absolutely impos-  
<!-- AA-p014-l017 -->
sible. However, if the player is simpiy being prevented from castling, but other  
<!-- AA-p014-l018 -->
squares are not covered, the movement of the king is only relatively restricted; in  
<!-- AA-p014-l019 -->
other words, it can move at the cost of losing the right to castle. Castling can also  
<!-- AA-p014-l020 -->
be thwarted indirectly: for instance, if the king has to guard one of the pieces  
<!-- AA-p014-l021 -->
which is protecting it (e.g. on e7 in Black's case).  
<!-- AA-p014-l022 -->
The second condition for an attack of this kind depends on the attacker's own  
<!-- AA-p014-l023 -->
circumstances. First of all, the e-file should be open, or it should at any rate be in  
<!-- AA-p014-l024 -->
the attacker's power to open it; the attacker should also either have a piece which  
<!-- AA-p014-l025 -->
can control a file (a rook or queen) on the file or be able quickly to post one on it.  
<!-- AA-p014-l026 -->
Besides this, he usually needs to strengthen his pressure on the e-file, for instance  
<!-- AA-p014-l027 -->
by doubling rooks or by attacking one of his opponent's pieces which is on the  
<!-- AA-p014-l028 -->
file and protecting the king.  
<!-- AA-p014-l029 -->
From these necessary conditions it transpires that in an attack along the e-file  
<!-- AA-p014-l030 -->
there tends to be a chain of defence, and the attack is carried out against the cen-  
<!-- AA-p014-l031 -->
tral unit of the chain, that is, the piece protecting the king. If this piece is on the  
<!-- AA-p014-l032 -->
square directly in front of the king (e7 or e2), the attacker may be able to mate by
