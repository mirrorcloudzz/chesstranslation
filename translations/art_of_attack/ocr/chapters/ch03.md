<!-- AA-p051-l003 -->
The origin of castling  
<!-- AA-p051-l004 -->
Castling, the most distinctive move in the whole of chess, affects the nature of  
<!-- AA-p051-l005 -->
mating attacks in various ways and because of its importance in this connection,  
<!-- AA-p051-l006 -->
the general significance of the move is worth examining.  
<!-- AA-p051-l007 -->
Castling is not of any great antiquity. It is also obvious that such a complex and  
<!-- AA-p051-l008 -->
distinctive operation as the modern castling move was not born in a day, but  
<!-- AA-p051-l009 -->
rather developed gradually from century to century with the evolution of the  
<!-- AA-p051-l010 -->
rules of the game themselves.  
<!-- AA-p051-l011 -->
In the Indian game of chaturanga (pre-chess) there is no trace of the move, nor  
<!-- AA-p051-l012 -->
can it be found in the Arab shatranj. The first traces of any unusual or exceptional  
<!-- AA-p051-l013 -->
movement being made by the king, out of which the castling move evolved, are  
<!-- AA-p051-l014 -->
to be found in mediaeval European chess, which in its first stages can be de-  
<!-- AA-p051-l015 -->
scribed as the first and not very significant revision of shatranj towards providing  
<!-- AA-p051-l016 -->
the pieces with greater mobility. A historical source attesting to this first revision  
<!-- AA-p051-l017 -->
is a work by the Lombard monk Jacobus de Cessolis, in which are recorded the  
<!-- AA-p051-l018 -->
rules governing the movements of the individual chess pieces at the time. There it  
<!-- AA-p051-l019 -->
is stated that king, queen, and pawns have the right to make an initial move of two  
<!-- AA-p051-l020 -->
squares as well as the normal one. From this early reform the double opening  
<!-- AA-p051-l021 -->
step taken by the pawn survives to this day, while in the case of the queen it be-  
<!-- AA-p051-l022 -->
came obsolete when the piece was given its present powers of movement during  
<!-- AA-p051-l023 -->
the great reform of the game at the end of the fifteenth century. In the case of the  
<!-- AA-p051-l024 -->
king it was further developed and altered until it eventually took its present-day  
<!-- AA-p051-l025 -->
shape as castling.  
<!-- AA-p051-l026 -->
There is extensive confirmation of the introduction of the king's move from  
<!-- AA-p051-l027 -->
other sources after Cessolis, though the particular conditions governing the move  
<!-- AA-p051-l028 -->
vary. The form most frequently recorded is that where the king moves like a  
<!-- AA-p051-l029 -->
knight ('Freudensprung'), with the limitation that it is not allowed to go beyond  
<!-- AA-p051-l030 -->
the second rank. The next form, which made its appearance in Italy in the six-  
<!-- AA-p051-l031 -->
teenth century, already included a movement by the rook in the same move - a  
<!-- AA-p051-l032 -->
step nearer to the castling of today. The king's use of the knight's jump disap-  
<!-- AA-p051-l033 -->
pears at this point and the rules state that the king is free to move from e1 to g1 or

<!-- AA-p052-l004 -->

<!-- AA-p052-l005 -->

<!-- AA-p052-l007 -->
This short survey of the historical evolution of castling is a good illustration of  
<!-- AA-p052-l008 -->
the gradual development of the rules of chess and it makes it particularly clear  
<!-- AA-p052-l009 -->
that these rules have been changed and perfected with the desire of enriching the  
<!-- AA-p052-l010 -->
game; the general tendency towards giving the pieces more dynamic movement  
<!-- AA-p052-l011 -->
reflects the increased animation of social movements in Europe from the Middle  
<!-- AA-p052-l012 -->
Ages onwards.  
<!-- AA-p052-l013 -->
In addition to this general line of development in the laws of chess, we also  
<!-- AA-p052-l014 -->
owe the introduction and eventual adoption of the castling move to the indirect  
<!-- AA-p052-l015 -->
influence of the great reform of the fifteenth century. This reform created a pow-  
<!-- AA-p052-l016 -->
erful queen out of the fers (which moved one square) and the modern bishop out  
<!-- AA-p052-l017 -->
of the alfil (which moved exactly two squares). In this new type of chess, with  
<!-- AA-p052-l018 -->
three long-range pieces, the fighting ability of the king declined; once in the days  
<!-- AA-p052-l019 -->
of pre-chess it had been the strongest piece of all. The king now became a hin-  
<!-- AA-p052-l020 -->
drance with its slow movement, and its position in the centre of the back rank  
<!-- AA-p052-l021 -->
stood out immediately as a fault in the game's structure. On the one hand, it had  
<!-- AA-p052-l022 -->
to be safeguarded against the formidable new forces, and on the other it had to be  
<!-- AA-p052-l023 -->
removed from the centre so as not to hamper them. For this reason it was neces-  
<!-- AA-p052-l024 -->
sary for the king to get away from the centre as quickly as possible, and it was to  
<!-- AA-p052-l025 -->
this end that the king's double move was built on and transformed into the full  
<!-- AA-p052-l026 -->
castling move. Therefore, not only the development of this most distinctive move  
<!-- AA-p052-l027 -->
can be traced through the history of chess but also its logic: it exists as the neces-  
<!-- AA-p052-l028 -->
sary complement to the reform of the other pieces' movements. We start a game  
<!-- AA-p052-l029 -->
of chess today with the pieces placed in the ancient order which derives from  
<!-- AA-p052-l030 -->
chaturanga, and then we switch over by castling to a new position, which is better  
<!-- AA-p052-l031 -->
suited to the alterations made in the game's rules.  
<!-- AA-p052-l032 -->
The dynamic and positional significance of castling  
<!-- AA-p052-l033 -->
Chess moves in general can be looked at from the dynamic or the positional point  
<!-- AA-p052-l034 -->
of view, depending on whether we are considering them as part of an active com-  
<!-- AA-p052-l035 -->
bination or as static features.  
<!-- AA-p052-l036 -->
The castling move without doubt powerfully increases the dynamic potential  
<!-- AA-p052-l037 -->
of a position. A player who has castled at once obtains a whole range of possibili-  
<!-- AA-p052-l038 -->
ties which he did not have before. He has increased the influence of the rook,  
<!-- AA-p052-l039 -->
cleared the square el for a possible attack along the e-file, and on top of this he

<!-- AA-p053-l003 -->
has prepared the ground for the quick establishment of 'communication between  
<!-- AA-p053-l004 -->
the rooks', i.e. the entire clearing of the back rank, as a result of which the rooks  
<!-- AA-p053-l005 -->
cover each other or can combine together in attacks.  
<!-- AA-p053-l006 -->
On the other hand, castling is also a positional move, and one which provides a  
<!-- AA-p053-l007 -->
particular element of permanency to the position. For it is unique and irrevoca-  
<!-- AA-p053-l008 -->
ble; it can only be carried out once in a game, which means that the king will as a  
<!-- AA-p053-l009 -->
rule stay on the side on which it has castled. In special cases it can, it is true,  
<!-- AA-p053-l010 -->
travel with a greater or lesser degree of difficulty from its castled position to vari-  
<!-- AA-p053-l011 -->
Ous parts of the board. Thus castling is only relatively irrevocable (unlike a  
<!-- AA-p053-l012 -->
pawn's move, which is absolutely so) but for practical purposes the king's posi-  
<!-- AA-p053-l013 -->
tion after castling is, at least until the arrival of the endgame, fairly definitely a  
<!-- AA-p053-l014 -->
permanent positional feature. Before castling, the king has three possible courses  
<!-- AA-p053-l015 -->
of action: to remain in the middle or to castle either to right or left. Once it has  
<!-- AA-p053-l016 -->
castled it no longer has any choice; it then has a definite shelter, a 'permanent ad-  
<!-- AA-p053-l017 -->
dress', a fact which the opponent naturally takes into account. It is certainly safer  
<!-- AA-p053-l018 -->
against attack, but it has at the same time committed itself positionally to one par-  
<!-- AA-p053-l019 -->
ticular area of the board; thus the undoubted advantages of castling are offset by  
<!-- AA-p053-l020 -->
certain disadvantages.

The correct moment for castling  
<!-- AA-p054-l021 -->
Beginners are often advised to castle as quickly as possible. This is a useful and  
<!-- AA-p054-l022 -->
sensible piece of advice in the majority of instances. Certainly, less experienced  
<!-- AA-p054-l023 -->
players offend against this general precept extremely often, postponing castling  
<!-- AA-p054-l024 -->
unnecessarily and as a result suddenly finding themselves in an awkward situa-  
<!-- AA-p054-l025 -->
tion, by which time it is far too late to castle. The present writer once made a sta-  
<!-- AA-p054-l026 -->
tistical survey of late castling using a number of simultaneous games and came to  
<!-- AA-p054-l027 -->
the conclusion that a common characteristic of so-called 'simultaneous massa-  
<!-- AA-p054-l028 -->
cres' (when the master wins all, or nearly all, of the games) was precisely the  
<!-- AA-p054-l029 -->
lateness of castling. Around the twelfth move of one such simultaneous display  
<!-- AA-p054-l030 -->
the situation was as follows: the master had castled on eighteen of the boards and  
<!-- AA-p054-l031 -->
not yet on two, while his opponents had castled on three boards but not on the  
<!-- AA-p054-l032 -->
rest. The result: 20-0 to the master - a real massacre! This is a typical example  
<!-- AA-p054-l033 -->
which indicates the disadvantages resulting from a delay in castling. In other si-  
<!-- AA-p054-l034 -->
multaneous displays, where masters had a harder task and poorer results, the sta-  
<!-- AA-p054-l035 -->
tistics on castling, taken around the twelfth to fifteenth moves, on the whole  
<!-- AA-p054-l036 -->
showed smaller discrepancies.  
<!-- AA-p054-l037 -->
The general rule that one should castle as soon as possible is therefore quite in  
<!-- AA-p054-l038 -->
order, though it must at once be emphasized that there are also many exceptions  
<!-- AA-p054-l039 -->
to it. Every chess player of greater experience is well acquainted with those

The basic elements of the attack on the castled king  
<!-- AA-p059-l034 -->
The attack on the castled king is a particular type of mating attack, but in practice  
<!-- AA-p059-l035 -->
it is the most important, since castling takes place in the majority of games.  
<!-- AA-p059-l036 -->
In considering an attack of this kind, we must begin with a general survey of  
<!-- AA-p059-l037 -->
the action involved, acquainting ourselves, on the one hand, with the basic char-  
<!-- AA-p059-l038 -->
acteristics of the actual castled position itself and, on the other, with the good and  
<!-- AA-p059-l039 -->
bad aspects of an attack on that position.

The castled position consists of the castled king and the protective pawns and  
<!-- AA-p060-l004 -->

<!-- AA-p060-l005 -->

<!-- AA-p060-l006 -->

<!-- AA-p060-l007 -->

<!-- AA-p060-l008 -->

<!-- AA-p060-l009 -->

<!-- AA-p060-l010 -->

<!-- AA-p060-l011 -->

<!-- AA-p060-l012 -->

<!-- AA-p060-l013 -->
bishop.)  
<!-- AA-p060-l014 -->
The passive nature of the castled position results from the lack of mobility of  
<!-- AA-p060-l015 -->
the king, and this is indirectly transferred to the other pieces in the position as  
<!-- AA-p060-l016 -->

<!-- AA-p060-l017 -->
tled position is not to be weakened', and which are as a result made indirectly im-  
<!-- AA-p060-l018 -->
mobile. If they in fact move from their initial squares and so create weaknesses in  
<!-- AA-p060-l019 -->
the castled position, these weaknesses result from the reduced options entailed  
<!-- AA-p060-l020 -->
by the advance. (A pawn at h2 controls the square g3 and also has it in its power,  
<!-- AA-p060-l021 -->

<!-- AA-p060-l022 -->

<!-- AA-p060-l023 -->
part of its active potential.) Even the pieces associated with the castled position  
<!-- AA-p060-l024 -->

<!-- AA-p060-l025 -->
whether functionally because of the need for a certain square to be defended or  
<!-- AA-p060-l026 -->

<!-- AA-p060-l030 -->

<!-- AA-p060-l031 -->

<!-- AA-p060-l032 -->

<!-- AA-p060-l033 -->

<!-- AA-p060-l034 -->

<!-- AA-p060-l036 -->

<!-- AA-p060-l037 -->

<!-- AA-p060-l038 -->
