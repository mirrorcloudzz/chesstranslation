# B: Squares

## 3: Weak Squares on the Sixth (Third) Rank

## WS-1

One of the most tempting objectives of a plan is to create real weak squares on the sixth (third) rank. Establishing a knight or bishop on these squares usually represents a positional achievement just as decisive as obtaining an "eternal" knight. Sometimes the two ideas are combined.

We begin with just such an example.

The game Pillsbury-Chigorin (Vienna, 1898) reached the diagrammed position. White's knight has established itself on the weak b6-square. Under its cover White intends to attack the a5-pawn, after whose fall the passed a-pawn should have a decisive say.

Pillsbury, however, pursued this objective too directly:

29. Ra3

Chigorin immediately sacrificed the exchange in order to eliminate the "eternal" knight:

29... Rxb6! 30. cxb6 Qxb6

White has a material advantage, but Black has obtained definite counter-chances in connection with the weakness of the d4-pawn - a weak point.

The game continued:

31. Rf1 Rxd4

As White's reply shows, this does not win a pawn at all.

32. Rxa5 c5 33. Ra8 Kf8 34. Qh3 Ke7 35. Qh4+ f6 36. Qh8 Rd8 37. Qxg7+ Bf7 38. Rxd8 c4+ 39. Kh1 Qxd8 40. Rb1?

A presumptuous move which even leads to defeat. The correct 40. Qh6 Qd3 41. Rg1 Qd2 would have achieved only a draw.

40... c3!

Pillsbury must now give up a rook for this pawn, and he resigned a few moves later.

Returning to the diagrammed position, we must once again emphasise the powerful role of the knight on b6, which was probably quite sufficient for victory. Instead of 29. Ra3 it was necessary to play 29. Qe5, and after 29... Qa7, 30. Rb3, consolidating the knight's base.

## WS-2

In the following instructive example, both knights carry out a devastating raid on the sixth rank.

The position comes from Romanovsky-Smorodsky (3rd USSR Championship, 1924). White is conducting an offensive. His pawn group has already advanced to the fifth rank and severely cramps Black. White's c-pawn can advance to c5, but is it worth vacating c5 with the knight? The square is very weak, it lies in the enemy camp, and the knight together with the pawns exerts serious pressure on the centre and queenside. Thus the black rook on d6 has no move. ...Nd7 is also impossible because of Ne6 and c5. The black bishop is tied to the b7-pawn, since ...b6 is met by Nxa6.

White undoubtedly has several ways to realise his advantage. He chooses a plan of invading the sixth rank with the knights via d6 and b6. The knight on c5 therefore abandons its excellent post in order to penetrate even more deeply into the enemy position.

31. Nb3 Kf7 32. c5 R6d7

Some players might now be tempted by 33. d6, but that would only help Black free himself a little. There follows ...Ke8, then ...Rf7, while e6 becomes an excellent springboard for Black's bishop and knight. Moreover, by "blocking" d6 with a pawn, White deprives one of his knights of an excellent base.

33. Na5

The knight heads for d6 via c4.

33... Rc7 34. Rd1 h5

Black is helpless.

35. Rfd2 Rcd7 36. Na4

The beginning of a victorious nine-move knight attack.

36... Ke8 37. Nb6 Rc7 38. Nac4 Bd7 39. Nd6+ Ke7

The objective is achieved. The white cavalry holds the entire enemy army in a vice - a good example of how the strength of a knight increases when it enters the enemy camp on weak squares.

40. Nb5!

In view of the threat Nd6+, White wins at least the exchange and retains the initiative. Further resistance is useless, and Black soon resigned.

## WS-3

### Queen's Indian Defence

White: A. Alekhine  
Black: J. R. Capablanca  
New York, 1927

1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 e6 3. Nf3 b6 4. g3 Bb7 5. Bg2 c5 6. d5

This aggressive continuation was recommended in its day by Rubinstein. Great defensive accuracy is required against it. Black must take the pawn to avoid difficulties in developing his queenside forces, but this leads to an unpleasant pin of the d5-pawn. Exploiting this pin, White aims eventually to create a base for his pieces on d5. The whole variation leads to a sharp, tense struggle, in which the first exchange of pieces occurs only after 17 moves.

6... exd5 7. Nh4 g6 8. Nc3 Bg7 9. O-O O-O 10. Bf4

An interesting plan introduced by Alekhine, aiming to exploit the rather weak d6-point. For example, after 10... d6 11. cxd5, Black cannot immediately develop the knight on d7, while after ...Nh5 his influence in the centre is weakened and the knight is not particularly well placed.

More often employed here is 10. cxd5, followed by an advance of the central pawns, e4 and f4.

10... d6 11. cxd5 Nh5 12. Bd2

In retreating to d2 White is apparently guided by the desire to retain the possibility of advancing the central pawns, especially e4. Yet only three moves later he finds it necessary to transfer the bishop to e3 even though his pawn is still on e2. The reply 12. Be3 is more purposeful for other reasons as well. Black cannot display activity on the queenside without ...c4; with the bishop on e3, however, this would allow White to centralise it by Bd4.

12... Nd7 13. f4?

This move should be blamed not so much in itself - with consistent play it would not have led to particularly serious consequences - as for being the beginning of a false idea. It is incomprehensible why White refrains from 13. e4, threatening Nf5, and after 13... Nhf6, f4.

13... a6

Black's plan becomes clear: supported by the excellently placed bishop on g7, he intends to conduct a full-scale pawn offensive on the queenside. This plan is highly active.

14. Bf3

White prompts the knight to return to a better position. More consistent was 14. a4 followed by e4, or even 14. e4 at once, threatening Nf5. In that case Black would probably play ...Nhf6 himself.

14... Nhf6 15. a4?

Preventing ...b5, but appreciably weakening the queenside, which Black later manages to exploit. Even now, with 15. e4, White could still have fought successfully for the initiative. For example, 15. e4 b5 16. Qc2, and if 16... b4, then 17. Nd1!, threatening Nd1-e3-c4. If 16... c4, then 17. Rae1 Re8 18. Nd1, and White's purposeful concentration in the centre with the advance e4-e5 in view must cause Black anxiety.

15... c4

This move fixes the weak b3-square and, after the e-pawn advances, also d3, since the black knight has a direct route to this point via c5.

16. Be3 Qc7

Intending, after 17... Nc5 18. Bxc5, to recapture on c5 with the queen. Black's pieces operate very harmoniously; his rooks will take up a dominating position on the e-file, while White's d5-pawn is weak. Convinced that his position is bad, White launches a reckless attack on the kingside.

## WS-4

17. g4

White could have defended tenaciously with 17. Qc2 Nc5 18. Rad1 Rfe8 19. Bf2 Nb3 20. e4.

17... Nc5 18. g5 Nfd7 19. f5?

This gives Black another base, the e5-square, while accomplishing nothing for White.

It is known that on the day of this encounter with Capablanca, then world champion, Alekhine was extremely nervous, which perhaps explains his play. A chess player's condition and even his character are closely connected with the style and quality of his play.

White continues to play as if everything were already lost. With 19. Qe1, followed by Rd1 and Qf2, he could have defended successfully, reserving f5 for a suitable moment.

19... Rfe8 20. Bf4 Be5 21. Bg4 Nb3 22. fxg6 hxg6 23. Rb1 Bxc3 24. bxc3 Qc5+ 25. e3

Of course not 25. Kg2 or 25. Kh1, when Black captures the d5-pawn with check. But now d3 is weakened, and Black immediately exploits it.

25... Ne5 26. Bf3 Nd3!

Black need not hurry to take the pawn. Supported by the c4-pawn, the knights are picturesquely perched on d3 and b3. This elegant picture repeats the arrangement seen in the previous example, but here the background - the positional circumstances - is more complicated and structurally colourful.

White must relieve his position at all costs, but the exposed king does not allow him to avert serious loss in the approaching crisis.

27. Kh1 Bxd5 28. Rxb3 Nxf4 29. Rb1 Rxe3 30. Ng2 Rxf3! 31. Rxf3 Nxg2 32. Kxg2 Re8 33. Kf1 Bxf3 34. Qxf3 Qxg5 35. Re1

35. Rxb6 is impossible because of 35... Qc1+ and 36... Qd2+.

35... Rxe1+

Even in the queen ending, resistance three pawns down is useless. White resigned on the 42nd move.

## WS-5

A bishop's invasion and consolidation on the sixth (third) rank, particularly on e6, d6 (e3, d3), is likewise a considerable and at times decisive positional achievement.

The ending of the second game of the 1908 world championship match between Emanuel Lasker and Tarrasch is a classic example of this plan. Lasker, with Black, played the opening poorly, lost a pawn, and seemed doomed to defeat. Thereafter, however, thanks to indifferent play by his opponent, he succeeded in beginning a counter-struggle in the centre around White's isolated e4-pawn.

Black has just played 24... d5 and threatens not only 25... dxe4 26. Nxe4 f5 but also simply 25... Qg6. White's correct continuation was 25. Nf5, so that after 25... dxe4 he could reply 26. Nxh6. If the bishop retreats, 26. exd5 is possible, provoking an exchange of rooks. The knight occupies an excellent position on f5, and after 25... Bf8 - for example, 26. exd5 Bc5+ 27. Kf1 Qg6 28. Rxe5 Rxe5 29. g4 - it is difficult for Black to continue the attack.

Tarrasch, however, immediately takes on d5 and exposes e3, which Lasker energetically exploits.

25. exd5? Be3+ 26. Kf1 cxd5 27. Rd3

White underestimates the role of the bishop on e3. Even now he should continue 27. Nf5 d4 28. Nxe3 dxe3 29. Re2, accepting a cramped but far from hopeless position.

27... Qe6 28. Re2 f5 29. Nh1 f4 30. Rd1 d4

The power of the bishop is now revealed in all its brilliance. Under its cover and with its assistance, Black's attack proceeds swiftly and unhindered towards its goal.

31. Nf2 Qa6 32. Nd3 Rg5 33. Ra1 Qh6 34. Ke1

After 34. h3, 34... Rg3 35. Qd5 f3 is decisive.

34... Qxh2 35. Kd1 Qg1+ 36. Ne1

Or 36. Re1 Qxg2.

36... Rge5 37. Qc6 R5e6 38. Qxc7 R8e7 39. Qd8+ Kg7 40. a4 f3 41. gxf3 Bg5 0-1

After 42. Rxe6 Rxe6 43. Qa5 Bh4, White suffers serious material loss.

## WS-6

The following position arose in Alekhine-Mikenas at the 5th Olympiad (1933).

Black has a weak d6-square, towards which the enemy bishop is clearly heading. Mikenas, however, ignored the threat and continued:

12... a6

Apparently intending to attack the centre with ...c5 and meanwhile preventing the knight's invasion on b5.

Alekhine's comment on 12... a6 is interesting: "Black does not take into account that he must defend d6 at any price. From now on the dominating position of White's dark-squared bishop proves in itself a sufficient advantage to decide the outcome of the struggle." In Alekhine's opinion, ...Nf6 followed by ...Ne8 was necessary.

Further events developed as follows:

13. Bd6 f5

This move, which assists the consolidation of the bishop on d6, also demonstrates Black's incorrect assessment of the position. It was still useful to carry out the manoeuvre Nd7-f6-e8, for example 13... Nf6 14. e5 Ne8 15. Ba3 Rg8 16. h4 Nf5, although even then Black's position remains difficult because of the inactive queenside.

14. e5 Rg8 15. h4 b6

So that after 16... Nf8 17. Qg5, Black has the reply 17... Ra7.

16. Ne2 Nf8 17. a5 b5 18. g3 Rh8 19. Kg2 Kg8 20. Rh1 Kf7 21. Nf4 Rg8 22. b3 Nh7 23. c4 Bd7 24. Rac1 Bf8 25. Be2 Nc8 26. cxd5 cxd5 27. Bxf8

After the exchange of bishops, several weak dark squares arise in Black's camp - b6, f6 and especially c5. The last serves until the end of the game as an excellent base for White's pieces. In addition, White's rooks and queen soon occupy the c-file, which becomes a road of invasion into the enemy camp.

All these achievements are to a considerable extent consequences of the powerful positional pressure exerted by the bishop on d6 over 13 moves. It not only cramped Black and played an important role in the development of the initiative, but also forced him into a series of moves that weakened his position.

Since we will soon examine the role of open lines in a plan, we give the end of the game as a brief introduction to this important strategic theme.

27... Nxf8 28. Rc5

Thus not only the knight and bishop, to which we have paid particular attention in examining weak squares, but also the other pieces - rooks, queen and, in the endgame, even the king - can exert a decisive influence on the struggle when placed on weak points.

28... Na7 29. Nd3 Kg7 30. Rhc1 Rc8 31. Rxc8 Bxc8 32. Qc3 Kh7 33. Qc5

The invasion begins. Black may still repel one or another direct threat, but he is unable to oppose White's offensive on the c-file with a planned defence.

33... Rg7 34. Qb6! Qe7 35. Nc5 g5 36. hxg5 hxg5 37. Ne1, and Black soon resigned.

## WS-7

We examine one more example of a bishop invading the sixth rank in the enemy camp: Holmov-Livshin, 21st USSR Championship, 1954.

Two important positional features can be seen in White's arrangement: the bishop is established on the weak e6-square, while on the c-file the white rooks attack the backward pawn, whose defence ties down Black's main forces. Were it not for this latter circumstance, Black might himself have exploited the open a-file to invade White's camp and to some extent compensate for the powerfully posted bishop on e6.

41. b5

Preventing the advance of the c-pawn once and for all.

41... Qe8

After 41... Nd8 42. e5 Nxe6 43. exd6 cxd6 44. dxe6, White would have had considerably better chances thanks to his control of the c-file and the passed pawn on e6. For example, 44... Rac7 is impossible: after both pairs of rooks are exchanged, White wins with e7.

42. Qb2 Qf8 43. Re3 Qa8 44. Re2

Black can no longer defend against the e5 break, whose strength is determined by the position of the bishop on e6.

44... Qf8 45. e5 Nd8

The main variation of the breakthrough is 45... dxe5 46. fxe5 Nxe5 47. Rxe5 fxe5 48. Rf1 Qd8 49. Qxe5+ Kh6 50. Qf4+ Kg7 51. Qf6+ Kh6 52. Rf4, and the mating threat is irresistible.

46. exd6 cxd6 47. Qd4 Rab7 48. g4 Re8 49. g5 Rbe7 50. f5

Opening lines on the kingside makes Black's resistance useless.

50... Rb7 51. Rf1 Kh8 52. gxf6 Nf7 53. fxg6 hxg6 54. Rg2 Qh6 55. Rg3 Kg8 56. Qd3 1-0

In the final position, the bishop on e6 plays the leading role, just as it did throughout the attack.
