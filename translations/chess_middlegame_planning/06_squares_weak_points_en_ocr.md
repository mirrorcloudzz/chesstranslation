# B: Squares

## 1: Weak Points

## WP-1

In the process of a chess game, weak and strong points are often formed in the positions of both opponents. A strong point is a square where one of the pieces establishes itself so firmly that it is difficult to drive it away or exchange it. A strong square for one side will, of course, be weak for the other.

We illustrate this statement with an example.

The d5 square is a strong point for White and a weak one for Black. The White knight established there cannot be driven off or exchanged by a piece of equal value, and its activity, extending to many squares in Black's camp, severely cramps him. With support from this knight it is easier for White to carry out an attack on the enemy king's position. In fact, the game from which this position is taken (Smyslov-Rudakovsky, 14th USSR Championship, 1945) ended in a quick defeat for Black:

18. c3 b5 19. b3 Qc5+ 20. Kh1 Rc8 21. Rf3 Kh8 22. f6! gxf6 23. Qh4 Rg8 24. Nxf6 Rg7 25. Rg3 Bxf6 26. Qxf6 Rg8 27. Rd1 d5 28. Rxg7 1-0

It is not hard to see that Black's main trouble was the impossibility of defending the d5 square while at the same time attacking it. Precisely this circumstance was his weakness.

## WP-2

If, in the diagrammed position, the Black pawn were not on d6 but on c7, that same point would cease to be weak, since Black could attack it by ...c6 and force the knight to retreat. The same would apply if Black had a light-squared bishop or a knight instead of a dark-squared bishop and consequently could exchange the knight established on d5.

Thus the potential weakness of a square arises from the impossibility of attacking it with pawns. Such a square, however, should be considered a real weakness only when an enemy piece threatens to occupy it and cannot then be driven away or eliminated by exchange.

A weak square is at the same time a weak point, but the concept of a "weak point" applies not only to a weak square.

A weak point may also be a pawn which is threatened with attack by enemy pieces and is very difficult to defend.

## WP-3

In this position, depicting a moment from the game Zubarev-Rubinstein (Moscow, 1925), the e2 square, occupied by a backward White pawn, is a weak point. The e2-pawn is attacked by three pieces and defended by only two. It is important to mention that the e3 point in White's position is also weak, which makes the move e4 impossible. On 34. Ne4 follows ...f5. After 34. g4 Qh4 35. Kg2 Re3! 36. Rac1 f5 37. gxf5 gxf5, White loses the f4-pawn and also comes under an irresistible attack. Zubarev prefers to give up the e2-pawn at once, but this also does not save him from swift ruin:

34. Qd1 Rxe2 35. Rxe2 Qxe2 36. Qxa4 Re6 37. Rb1 Qf3 38. Qd1 Re2 39. Qf1 c4 40. b4 cxd3 41. Rb3 Rxf2 0-1

If on the 40th move he had played 40. dxc4, then of course 40... d3 would have followed, with the irresistible threat ...Bd4.

A weak point may also be a pawn which is not threatened for the time being, but which later may become an object of attack by the opponent.

Thus, in the starting position, the f2 and f7 points are weaker than the others, since in a number of openings they systematically become objects of attack; at the same time these points cannot be regarded as weak squares.

A weak square and a weak point will sometimes be temporary features, since the majority of chess processes proceed dynamically and the conditions of the struggle are constantly changing.
