# B: Squares

## 2: The "Eternal" Knight

## EK-1

When a knight occupies an impregnable position on a weak square in the centre or in the opponent's camp, it becomes particularly strong. Not for nothing do we give such a piece the graphic name "eternal" knight.

We will look at a few examples in which the "eternal" knight decides the outcome of the struggle.

The following position arose after the move 18. Qe3 in the game Izmailov-Kasparyan, played in the semi-final of the 7th USSR Championship (1931).

18... Bxf3

Also possible was 18... Bf5, when 19. g4? Nf4 is bad for White, since the knight firmly occupies the weak square. White would have to reply 19. Nh4, so that after 19... Nf4 he could boldly continue 20. O-O-O.

19. Qxf3

After capturing with the queen, White must play accurately to prevent the opponent from obtaining an "eternal" knight by transferring it along the route ...Ng7-f5-d4. After 19. Bxf3 Ng7 20. Be4, Black would hardly have achieved this objective.

19... Qe7 20. Qe4 Ng7 21. h4 Nf5 22. h5?

A weak move which delays the knight's invasion of d4 only briefly. White could have obtained counter-chances with 22. Bg4 Nd4 23. O-O-O, or the quieter 23. O-O, preparing f4.

22... Qg5 23. Rd1 Rf8 24. Qg4

White should not head for an endgame, in which under the present circumstances it is much easier for Black to realise the advantage of the "eternal" knight.

24... Qxg4 25. Bxg4 Nd4

The objective has now been achieved!

26. Kf1 a4 27. a3 c5 28. Rd2 b5 29. cxb5 Nxb5 30. Be6+ Kg7 31. hxg6 hxg6 32. Kg2 Nd4 33. Bg4 Rfb8 34. Re1 Ra7 35. Bd1 Kf6 36. f4 exf4 37. gxf4 Rh8

Threatening ...Rah7 with a dangerous attack. White has to go in for further exchanges.

38. Rh1 Rxh1 39. Kxh1 Kf5 40. Rg2 Kxf4 41. Rxg6 Ke5 42. Rg5+ Ke4 43. Rh5 Ra8 44. Kg1 Rb8 45. Bxa4 Rxb2 46. Kf1 Ke3 47. Rh3+ Nf3 0-1

## EK-2

### Spanish Game

White: G. Levenfish  
Black: P. Dubinin  
9th USSR Championship, 1934

1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nf6 3. Nc3 Nc6 4. Bb5 d6

By a transposition of moves, a variation of the Steinitz Defence with the knight on f6 has been obtained. In this variation Black gets a rather cramped position, but practice has shown that it is difficult for White to exploit the small advantage gained in the centre.

5. d4 exd4

An unjustified exchange. The correct reply was 5... Bd7. True, Black would later have to exchange on d4 in that case as well, but only after mobilising his principal forces and preparing to castle.

6. Nxd4 Bd7 7. Bxc6 bxc6

Black endeavours to retain his light-squared bishop. After 7... Bxc6 8. Nxc6 bxc6 9. Qf3 Qd7 10. h3 Be7 11. Bf4 O-O 12. O-O, followed by Rad1 and Rfe1, it is difficult for him to withstand the concentrated attack of all the opponent's pieces.

8. Qf3

This active development of the queen, with the potential threat e5, has become possible only as a consequence of Black's fifth move.

8... c5

If 8... Be7, then 9. e5 dxe5 10. Nxc6 Bxc6 11. Qxc6+ Nd7 12. Nd5 Bd6 13. Bg5 f6 14. Be3 O-O 15. O-O, and by following with Rd1 White consolidates his achievement in the centre. The c6 and d5 squares are real weaknesses.

9. Nf5 Bxf5

All the same, Black has to exchange the light-squared bishop. The knight on f5 is very strongly posted and prevents castling, and consequently also the mobilisation of Black's forces. It cannot be driven back by 9... g6 because of 10. Bg5 gxf5 11. e5 dxe5 12. Bxf6 e4 13. Nxe4. These variations show the strength of the queen's position on f3 before Black has castled.

10. Qxf5 Qd7 11. Qf3 Rb8 12. O-O Be7 13. b3 O-O

Black must now keep the light squares under constant control. The principal role in carrying out this task falls to the knight, so he must avoid exchanging it for the enemy bishop for as long as possible. Otherwise the task of defence would fall entirely upon the pawns. For example, ...c6 is required to defend d5, but it severely weakens d6; ...g6, defending f5, creates new weaknesses in the castled position. From this point of view Black's last move is inaccurate. The exchange of bishop for knight enters into White's plan, and it would have been useful to forestall it with 13... h6.

## EK-3

14. Bg5

White consistently carries out his plan. True, he does not yet threaten to take the knight, but after 15. Rad1 the threat of exchange becomes unpleasant.

14... Nd5?

Astonishingly, Black himself meets his opponent's wishes! If the knight is to move, 14... Ne8 is better, although this too breaks the communication between the black rooks. Nevertheless, after 14... Ne8 15. Bxe7 Qxe7 16. Nd5 Qe6, Black could defend himself with some success, retaining the possibility of ...c6, while the king's rook enters the game by ...f6 and ...Rf7. In this defensive plan the knight would play a major role, covering c7, d6 and g7.

There was another way to counter White's plan: 14... h6 and, after 15. Bh4, 15... g5 16. Bg3 Rfe8. The drawback is the considerable weakening of the kingside, especially f5, but in return Black would obtain an object of attack in the e4-pawn.

15. Nxd5 Bxg5 16. Qg3 Bd8 17. Rad1 f5?

Another mistake, after which the white knight on d5 becomes "eternal". Black's position is admittedly very difficult. After 17... c6 18. Ne3 the weakness on d6 is irreparable; yet without ...c6 it is difficult for Black to develop play, since the knight on d5 paralyses all his activity.

18. e5! Re8

After 18... c6 19. Nf6+ Bxf6 20. Rxd6 Qc7 21. exf6 Rxf6 22. Rxf6, the ending is miserable for Black, but this was the lesser evil.

19. exd6 cxd6

White has successfully completed the first part of his plan: he has an "eternal" knight in the centre. This advantage is fully sufficient for victory.

The struggle now approaches the concluding stage, in which the advantage is realised. Supported by the powerful position of the "eternal" knight, White develops an irresistible attack on the kingside.

20. Rfe1 Re6 21. Nf4 Rxe1+ 22. Rxe1 Ba5 23. Nd5 Bd8 24. h4 Rb7 25. c4 Kf7 26. Qf3 Kg8 27. Qe2

Threatening to win the bishop with Qe8+. The reply 27... Bxh4 is impossible because of 28. Qh5.

27... g6 28. h5 Kf8 29. h6

Intending Qb2. There were other ways as well, for example 29. Qb2 at once, or 29. hxg6 hxg6 30. Nf4.

29... Bg5

Black does not even stop the threat. His last hope was 29... Qf7, but after 30. Qd3 a5 31. Nf4 he would not have been able to resist for long either.

30. Qb2 Kg8 31. f4 Bd8 32. Nf6+

A beautiful conclusion! Of course, Re7 or Ne7+ was also sufficient.

32... Bxf6 33. Qxf6 d5 34. cxd5 1-0

After 34... Rb6 follows 35. Re8+.

## EK-4

In the 16th game of the 1951 world championship match between Bronstein and Botvinnik, the following position was reached.

Without examining the concrete purpose of Black's last move, 46... Qe8, Bronstein played:

47. Qd3?

There followed:

47... Bxf4 48. exf4 Bh5!

Bronstein was faced with a difficult choice. After 49. Bg2 Qa8, an invasion by the enemy pieces on the a-file is threatened, while the knight occupies a menacing central position on e4. After 49. Bxe4 dxe4 50. Qa3 Bf3+ 51. Kg1 Qh5 52. Qe3 Qh3 53. Qf2 h5, the threat ...h4 is irresistible.

Bronstein decides to allow Black an "eternal" knight, while trying to obtain some compensation by occupying the a-file.

49. Qa3 Bxf3+ 50. Rxf3 Rg7

Defending the b7-pawn against a possible attack by the queen. There is nothing in 50... Qa8, since White will not exchange but will reply 51. Rd3, adhering to waiting tactics. The tactical diversion 50... Qh5 likewise leads to nothing after 51. Kg2.

Black's plan is first to create a few more weak points in the opponent's camp, for example by provoking h4; then, by attacking them, to divert White's pieces from defence of the a-file; and finally to occupy this important strategic avenue of invasion. With the support of the "eternal" knight, it will then not be difficult to deliver the concluding blow.

51. Kg2 Qd8 52. Kf1 Qf6 53. Rd3 h5 54. h4

Of course White cannot allow ...h4.

54... Rg8

Intending, after ...Qg7, to keep the d4-pawn under attack and create the threat of invasion via g4.

55. Rd1

This somewhat eases Black's realisation of his positional advantage, but what can White do? If 55. Qa7, then 55... Qg7 and White is in zugzwang. 56. Qb6 is impossible because of 56... Ra8, while retreating the queen along the a-file is met by ...Qg4.

55... Qg7 56. Qf3 Kh6 57. Kg2 Ra8, and Black won.

## EK-5

The struggle was decided by the "eternal" knight in the game Terpugov-Bronstein (19th USSR Championship, 1951):

30. Rb2 Ndc5 31. Nxc5 Qxc5 32. f6?

Not only a useless move, since the queen's raid Qh6 is repulsed by ...Qf8, but also a serious positional miscalculation. It is clear that Black dreams of transferring the knight to the invulnerable d4-square. With his last move White himself removes control from e6, a transit point on the route to this cherished objective. It is not surprising that he soon suffers defeat.

Yet White's position was not altogether bad. Several active possibilities were available. Worth considering, for example, was the advance of the h-pawn. After 32. h4 it is dangerous for Black to take the c4-pawn with the queen because of 33. Rc1. White intends to continue h5, Bh3 and Rf1, applying pressure on the kingside.

32... Nc7 33. Ra4 Na6 34. Qh6

Another unnecessary thrust, helping Black carry out his plan even sooner. The rook is badly placed on a4, as soon becomes clear; it should simply have retraced its steps.

34... Qf8 35. Qg5

The exchange of queens simplifies the struggle to Black's advantage. The f6-pawn is very weak, and the plan of invading d4 is not easy to realise, so White naturally tries to complicate matters as much as possible on the kingside.

35... Nc5!

The poor position of White's rook on a4 makes itself felt. It must go back, since 36. Raxb4 Rxb4 37. Rxb4 loses the exchange after 37... Nxe4.

36. Ra1 h6 37. Qe3

Of course 37. Qxe5 is impossible because of 37... Nd3.

37... Kh7 38. Rd2 Ne6 39. a6 Rba7 40. Rda2 c5 41. h4 Qd6

On top of everything, White now has trouble with the a6-pawn.

42. h5 g5 43. Qf2 Nd4

The knight every chess player dreams of!

44. Rd2 Kg8

Black intends, after 45... Rxa6 46. Rxa6, to recapture on a6 with the rook; at present that is impossible because of Rxd4, followed by Qf5-c8+ with a draw.

45. Rda2

White sealed this move, but then resigned without resuming play. The decision was correct, since after ...Rxa6 the b-pawn, supported by the "eternal" knight, reaches the first rank without difficulty.
