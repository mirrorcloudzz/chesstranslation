## CH1-1

### Two victories of Wilhelm Steinitz

#### The main points of his creativity and technique

In the present chapter, we present two games which brilliantly reflect the chess views of Steinitz—the first strategist-theoretician of the chess struggle. However, we will deliberately illustrate the strategy of Steinitz, not on the basis of his theoretical reflections, which unfortunately sometimes bear a doctrinaire character, but on the basis of his practice, in the hope that the enormous creative canvas of the great master's chess thoughts will prompt the reader once more to go deeply into the essence and details of the process of planning, to the investigation of which the following chapters of this book are devoted.

In both cases, Steinitz's opponent was Emanuel Lasker.

## CH1-2

### Spanish Game

White: W. Steinitz  
Black: Em. Lasker  
(2nd match game, 1894)

`1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bb5 Nf6 4. d3`

Already this modest opening move hides within itself the seed of a great strategic plan, which is consistently and clearly realised both in the present game and in a number of other chess productions by Steinitz. Its essence lies in the creation of a firm centre by consolidating the e4-pawn and, on this basis, organising an energetic attack on the king's flank. For these objectives the queen's knight makes its way along the route Nd2-c4(f1)-e3-f5—a manoeuvre which Steinitz worked out and introduced into widespread practice. Together with this, he sheltered the king on the queen's flank, though in some cases, as in the present game, it remains in the centre.

Thus, these opening moves illustrate a definite purposefulness in Steinitz's thinking, extending far beyond the bounds of the opening stage. Here Steinitz elucidated two important creative principles which also guide present-day masters: a flank attack must be prepared by gaining control of, or firmly consolidating, the centre; the basic middlegame plan should already be laid down in the opening.

`4... d6 5. c3 Bd7`

By unpinning the knight and then developing the king's bishop on g7, Lasker apparently strives to create as much pressure as possible on the d4-point. However, Steinitz does not intend to start a battle in the centre, and therefore the arrangement of forces intended by Lasker proves insufficiently active.

Meanwhile, four years later, in the match game Gunsberg–Chigorin, an interesting plan of defence was demonstrated for Black, consisting of a very rapid preparation of the central advance ...d5.

`6. Ba4`

The bishop is transferred to b3 for an attack on the king's flank, or to c2 with the aim of consolidating the centre.

`6... g6 7. Nbd2 Bg7 8. Nc4 O-O 9. Ne3 Ne7 10. Bb3 c6`

Black has to spend a few more tempi preparing ...d5, which serves as an introduction to operations in the centre. This immediately allows Steinitz to show his “cards”.

## CH1-3

`11. h4!`

This tactical method—the rook's pawn making very rapid contact with the opponent's advanced knight's pawn, thereby opening an attacking line against the king—is likewise one of the modern methods of operation.

Beginning with this move, Steinitz sets about realising the basic part of his plan: a direct attack on Black's castled position.

`11... Qc7 12. Ng5`

White threatens, after `13. h5 Nxh5`, to sacrifice the exchange with `14. Rxh5`.

`12... d5`

Lasker likewise had the reply `12... h6` within his sights, but rejected it in view of `13. g4 hxg5 14. hxg5 Nh7 15. Nf5 gxf5 16. gxf5 Nxf5 17. Qh5`, etc. However, Black could defend successfully by `17... Nh6 18. gxh6 Bf6 19. Bg5! Qd8!` (if the knight or bishop takes on g5, then `20. Qg6+`, with unavoidable mate on g7) `20. Rg1 Kh8`.

To be fair, one should add that after `12... h6` White could continue the attack by `13. Qf3`. After `13... hxg5 14. hxg5 Nh7` (or `14... Nh5`) an intricate position arises, requiring great inventiveness and lofty technique in conducting both attack and defence. In any case, neither Lasker nor Steinitz could have envisaged all the numerous variations over the board.

In the first period of his chess career, Lasker avoided those continuations where it was impossible to control events. Steinitz, however, was much more willing to take risks, particularly when by doing so he could to some extent demonstrate his positional principles.

The whole question of how far one may take risks on the basis of a general evaluation of the position remains unresolved from Steinitz's time to the present day.

## CH1-4

`13. f3! Rad8 14. g4! dxe4`

Now `14... h6` no longer creates serious complications for White, in view of `15. Qe2 hxg5 16. hxg5 Nh7` (or `16... Ne8 17. Qh2 f6 18. exd5 cxd5 19. Nxd5 Nxd5 20. Bxd5+ Rf7 21. Qh7+ Kf8 22. Qxg6 Bc6 23. Qxf7+ Qxf7 24. Bxf7 Kxf7 25. Ke2`, and White has a rook and three pawns for a bishop and knight) `17. Nf5 gxf5 18. Qh2 Re8 19. Qxh7+ Kf8 20. gxf5`, and White has an irresistible attack.

`15. fxe4 h6`

All the same, Lasker has to resort to this move. Besides a direct attack by Qf3 or h5, White threatened to castle queenside after Qe2 and Bd2 and introduce the queen's rook into the battle. Yet Lasker's chosen way does not achieve its aim, while the position of the black king proves compromised.

The opponent would have faced greater difficulties after `15... Qc8`, attacking the g4-pawn for a third time, and if `16. Rg1`, then only now `16... h6`. To support the attack, White would have had to sacrifice both knights: `17. Qf3 hxg5 18. hxg5 Nh7 19. Nf5! gxf5 20. gxf5 Nxf5 21. exf5 Bxf5 22. g6 Nf6 23. gxf7+ Rxf7` (worse is `23... Kh8 24. Qg3`) `24. Bh6 Ne8` (`24... Be6` is impossible because of `25. Rxg7+ Rxg7 26. Qxf6`) `25. Bxf7+ Kxf7 26. Qh5+ Kg8 27. O-O-O`. With the threat of an exchange sacrifice on g7, as well as Rg5 or Rdf1, White creates a dangerous attack.

`16. Qf3! Be8`

The knight, as before, cannot be taken, since after `16... hxg5 17. hxg5 Nh7 18. Nf5! gxf5 19. Qh3 Re8 20. Qxh7+ Kf8 21. gxf5`, Black might as well resign.

`17. Bc2 Nd7 18. Nh3`

A judicious retreat, which nevertheless also contains an idea for the further development of the attack.

`18... Nc5 19. Nf2`

White's last move, consolidating the d3-point, testifies to Steinitz's strict adherence to the principle he formulated: a flank attack can succeed only with a firm centre.

The range of activity assigned to the white pieces in this plan is interesting. Only three moves ago, the bishop on b3 and the knight on g5 were spearheaded against f7. Now they switch completely to a defensive function. Great flexibility in manoeuvring the pieces was a characteristic trait of Steinitz's play. At the same time, it cannot escape notice that Steinitz retains an attacking position on the king's flank and, as further events prove, even decides to launch an attack without mobilising his reserves on the queen's flank. After Lasker's next move, he simply refrains from bringing these reserves into the battle.

## CH1-5

`19... b5`

A very cunning idea, aimed at creating a counterattack in the centre and on the queen's flank, and counting on the natural `20. Bd2`. In that case there would follow `20... b4 21. cxb4` (or `21. O-O-O bxc3 22. bxc3 Rb8`, with initiative on the queen's flank) `21... Ne6! 22. Bc3 c5 23. bxc5 Nc6!`, or `23. b5 Nd4`, completely refuting White's plan. Steinitz unravels Lasker's idea and immediately hurls himself into an attack founded on a bold and beautiful knight sacrifice.

`20. g5 h5 21. Nf5! gxf5`

Now Black has to take the knight, since passive defence offers no hope at all. Apart from accepting the sacrifice, the only active possibility was `21... f6`, but this reply is impossible: `22. Nxg7 Kxg7` (`22... fxg5 23. Nxe8`) `23. gxf6+ Rxf6 24. Bh6+ Kf7 25. Qe3`, and Black's position is bad.

`22. exf5 f6 23. g6 Nxg6`

Obviously forced, in view of the threat Qxh5. As a result of the first “bloodshed”, Black has gained a pawn. But an open g-file has appeared on the board, and White will control it. The g-file is a direct path leading to the king's own “palace”. It is precisely this circumstance that is especially threatening for Black.

`24. fxg6 Bxg6 25. Rg1`

How is Black to defend? A retreat of the bishop is met by `26. Bh6`; after `25... Kh7`, there is `26. Rxg6`, followed by d4+. If, for example, `25... Qf7`, then `26. Qg3 Bh7 27. Bh6 Rd7` (`27... Ne6 28. Bb3`) `28. Bxg7 Qxg7 29. Qh2 Bg6 30. d4`, with material gain.

`25... e4`

An interesting attempt to save the position was `25... Bxd3!`—a move to which Chigorin drew attention in his day. After `26. Bxd3 Rxd3! 27. Nxd3 e4 28. Qxh5 Nxd3+`, Black has good chances of at least a draw. If White answers `25... Bxd3` with `26. Bh6`, then `26... Rf7 27. Bxg7 Rxg7 28. O-O-O Bxc2`, and White cannot extract anything from the exposed position of the black king.

After missing this possibility, whose existence testifies to the complexity of the struggle, Lasker suffers material loss, after which the game largely enters a technical phase.

## CH1-6

`26. dxe4 Kh7`

Or `26... Qf7 27. b4 Ne6 28. Bb3 Rfe8 29. Nh3`, with the knight invading on f4.

`27. Rxg6! Kxg6 28. Qf5+ Kf7 29. Qxh5+ Kg8 30. Qxc5`

White not only has a bishop, knight and pawn for the rook—which in itself is sufficient for victory—but also creates direct threats. His two bishops operate very powerfully. The crushing `31. Bb3+` is now threatened.

`30... Qe5 31. Be3 a6 32. a4`

Bringing the queenside reserves into the battle immediately decides the game.

`32... Rfe8 33. axb5 axb5 34. Qxe5 Rxe5 35. Ra6`

Invasion! Curiously, the energetic introduction of the rook on a6 is its first and last action in this game. One move only! The rook paralyses the opponent's forces and thereby proves a powerful support for the concluding attack by White's minor pieces. It is also interesting that the king's rook makes only two moves in all: Rg1xg6.

Minimum strength, maximum achievement! This is the principle of economy which Steinitz repeatedly discussed in his theoretical works and demonstrated many times in his own games.

`35... Rc8 36. Ng4 Re7 37. Bc5 Ree8 38. Ne3 Bf8 39. Bd4 Kf7 40. h5 Be7 41. Bb3+ Kf8 42. Nf5`

In conclusion the “eternal knight” appears on the scene. Under its cover, and with the assistance of the excellently placed bishop, a free path opens for the h-pawn to the cherished h8-square. Therefore, `1-0`.

## CH1-7

### Queen's Gambit

White: W. Steinitz  
Black: Em. Lasker  
(St Petersburg, 1895/96)

`1. d4`

Steinitz inflicted a great many defeats on his contemporaries with this opening. This was of course not due to the first move itself, but to the well-composed, purposeful plans which Steinitz persistently and logically carried out in this opening.

`1... d5 2. c4 e6 3. Nc3 Nf6 4. Bf4`

Steinitz also played `4. Bg5` here—a move he employed as far back as 1873 at the international tournament in Vienna against Anderssen.

`4... Be7`

A colourless reply which ignores the opponent's plan and allows White, as in the previous game, to create a firm centre and then launch an energetic attack on the flank.

Meanwhile, Black had at least two active replies to Steinitz's plan: an immediate ...c5, undermining White's influence over e5, and the well-known attack on c3 by ...c6, ...Qa5 and ...Bb4. The latter appears in opening theory under the name of the Cambridge Springs system and gains still more force with the bishop on f4 instead of g5.

`5. e3 O-O 6. c5!`

White's plan, based on `4. Bf4`, becomes quite distinct. He prevents the counterattack ...c5 and establishes a solid formation in the centre.

In advancing the pawn to c5, Steinitz had to weigh carefully the opponent's undermining moves ...b6 and ...e5. He was probably convinced by the variation `6... b6 7. b4 a5 8. a3`, when Black cannot loosen the advanced post on c5.

`6... Ne4`

Black threatens, after `7. Nf3` or `7. Bd3`, to exchange on c3 and then begin undermining by ...b6. If White exchanges on e4 himself, Black prevents the development of White's knight on f3 and thereby weakens his pressure on e5. Yet Lasker's reasoning is not particularly far-sighted. Black's pawn, transferred to e4, becomes the object of the f3 break. Besides this, c4 is freed for the white bishop, where it takes up a convenient attacking position.

Black should concentrate his attention on the central e5-point. For this purpose it was necessary to play `6... c6`, preparing to develop the queen's knight on d7 (if at once `6... Nbd7`, then `7. Nb5` forces the other knight to retreat to e8). The game might develop as follows: `6... c6 7. Nf3 Nbd7 8. Bd3 Nh5`, and then, according to circumstances, ...f5 or ...Nxf4.

## CH1-8

`7. Nxe4 dxe4 8. Qc2 f5 9. Bc4 Nc6 10. a3`

Alongside the purposeful execution of the main line of his plan, White displays healthy foresight. Black threatened to neutralise the dangerous bishop with ...Na5, to which White can now reply `11. Ba2`.

`10... Bf6 11. O-O-O Kh8`

This and the following move prepare the advance ...e5. Only in this way can Black do something against the f3 break.

`12. f3 Qe7!`

Very cleverly played, setting White a serious problem. After `13. fxe4` follows `13... e5`, and if `14. d5`, then `14... exf4 15. dxc6 Qxc5`, when Black already has the initiative.

`13. Bg3`

So that after `13... e5` White can reply `14. d5 Na5 15. Ba2`.

`13... f4`

This advance looks promising. Thus, if `14. Bxf4`, then `14... e5 15. dxe5 Nxe5 16. Qxe4 Bf5! 17. Qxf5 Nxc4`, with a winning attack. However, Steinitz has prepared a surprise for his opponent.

`14. Qxe4!`

A beautiful sacrifice which Black is forced to accept. White gets two pawns for the bishop, retains control of the centre by virtue of his inviolable, solid eight-pawn chain and, most importantly, obtains a very dangerous attack on the opponent's king in connection with the open h-file.

`14... fxg3 15. hxg3`

## CH1-9

`15... g6`

For defensive reasons, Black decides to give up another pawn. Indeed, after `15... h6` there follows `16. Bd3`, or `16. f4 Bd7 17. Nf3` and then g4. Also unsatisfactory is `15... g5`. In this case Steinitz, in his own words, intended `16. f4 g4 17. Ne2 Bd7 18. Qc2`, threatening e4-e5. Doubling the rooks on the h-file would also be threatened.

`16. Qxg6 Bd7`

`16... e5` is bad because of d5-d6.

`17. f4 Rf7 18. g4 Rg7 19. Qh6 Rxg4 20. Bd3 Rg7 21. Nf3 Qf7 22. g4 Rag8`

Black could not take the pawn, since after `23. Qxh7+` he loses the bishop on d7.

`23. g5 Bd8 24. Rh2!`

This is considerably stronger than winning the exchange by `24. g6`. The threat Rdh1 is irresistible.

`24... Rg6 25. Qh5 R6g7 26. Rdh1 Qxh5 27. Rxh5 Rf8 28. Rxh7+ Rxh7`

Or `28... Kg8 29. Rxg7+ Kxg7 30. Rxh7+`, and the bishop on d7 is lost.

`29. Rxh7+ Kg8 30. Rxd7 Rf7 31. Bc4! 1-0`

In view of the loss of a fourth pawn after `31... Rxd7 32. Bxe6+ Rf7 33. g6`.

The whole game might serve as an example of a concrete plan and the harmony of active forces in the attack on h7.

## CH1-10

Both games are distinguished by clear, purposeful thinking. In them, Steinitz's play speaks for itself. Nevertheless, in concluding this opening chapter, we wish to recall the skill with which Steinitz handled the pawns and how economically he expended the energy of his pieces.

Minimum effort with maximum achievement! Repeating this creative slogan of Steinitz when planning a game, one may say that, despite the passage of time, it remains inscribed on the creative banner of chess art even today.

Steinitz's handbook *The Modern Chess Instructor* gives us an interesting statement about pawns:

“The skilful handling of pawns constitutes one of the most important methods of activity in the chess struggle. The loss of a pawn is fatal in the majority of cases if it is not compensated by a strong—and in any case better than the opponent's—arrangement of pieces. Not only weak pawns, but also weak squares on which enemy pieces can be placed, frequently entail defeat. Only the ability to handle the pawns can avert such favourable positional features for the opponent.”

There is no need to discuss this thought, which is so thoroughly in keeping with our own times.
