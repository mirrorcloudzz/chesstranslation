# B: Squares

## 4: Some Conclusions

## SC-1

In summing up everything that has been said about weak squares, we may first of all conclude that they are positional elements capable of considerably increasing the strength of pieces - in the first place the bishop or knight - sometimes to an extent sufficient to decide the outcome of the game.

This conclusion brings us directly to the question of the relative strength of the pieces, which should be assessed according to the role they fulfil in a given concrete position.

Another conclusion concerns the causes of weakened squares, which can be divided into two categories: (i) structural and (ii) positional.

The structural cause is determined by the position of the pawns. A square which cannot be attacked by pawns may be considered structurally weak. It must once again be emphasised, however, that the weakness of such a square is only potential. It becomes a real weakness only when the opponent has the positional possibility of exploiting the square as a base for his pieces.

The absence of a bishop operating on squares of the same colour as the weakened square, or of a knight which powerfully defends that square, must likewise be regarded as a structural cause, since neither circumstance in itself determines the actual weakness of a square.

## SC-2

With the development of a bishop on the flank - for example, on g2 for White - the pawn structure e3-f2-g3-h2 frequently arises, in which the f3 and h3 squares are structurally weakened. Yet this circumstance in no way prevents contemporary opening theory from recommending the fianchetto of the bishop in a great many openings.

It is therefore clear that structural weaknesses as such should not be feared; otherwise we deprive ourselves of the possibility of carrying out many active plans. Nevertheless, in manoeuvring play it is useful to avoid even structural weaknesses when they are not properly motivated and well founded.
