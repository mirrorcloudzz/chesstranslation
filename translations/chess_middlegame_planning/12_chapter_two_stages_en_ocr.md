## CH2-1

### Stages of the Plan

#### Squares and lines as special-purpose objects of the plan. The preparatory stage. Concrete definition of a position. Realisation of successes achieved

Every plan can usually be subdivided into several consecutive stages, one following another. A single great plan can be broken up, as it were, into several small plans, each with its own objectives and ways of achieving them. It also happens that the course of events forces one of the opponents, and sometimes even both, to change their ideas radically.

As an example, we shall now try to sketch a picture of the execution of a three-stage plan.

In the first stage of the struggle, an object for the creative plan has to be created. This stage is characterised by the absence of a concrete objective and is such that—by provoking the advance of some of the opponent's pawns, for example—it bears a particular, one might say narrowly tactical, character. In this part of the plan the opponents must be guided by general considerations based on the fundamental positional principles of play. The task of both sides is to create weak squares in the opponent's camp and, when the opportunity presents itself, weak points. At the same time, it is necessary to counteract the opponent's execution of the same task. Thus, at the first stage of the plan, each side must combine active operations with prophylactic measures against the weakening of its own position.

In the second stage, the planned objective comes to light. The way of achieving it becomes increasingly clear, and the concrete definition of the game attains great depth.

If the objective is achieved, one side transfers the struggle to the third stage, which proceeds under the heading of realising the advantage obtained. This stage may be called technical, but only to a certain extent, since it frequently requires the ability to employ methods arising from theoretical laws and rules.

The opening represents a component part of the first stage. In several openings, particularly gambits, the process of struggle may also be regarded as the second stage of the plan. A single plan may have not only three stages, but four, five or even more. On the other hand, practice also knows one-stage plans. These occur most often in games where plans remain incomplete and conditions change, so that separate parts of the game prove to have no logical connection with one another.

## CH2-2

We devote the present chapter to plans in which weak squares and open lines serve as the principal purposeful objects.

Accordingly, a plan directed toward these objects may be subdivided into the following stages:

1. the struggle to create weak squares or open lines;
2. the struggle to seize or occupy the weak squares or lines;
3. the realisation of the advantage arising from the occupation of weak squares or open lines.

We begin with a game played as far back as the St Petersburg tournament of 1909.

### Spanish Game

White: R. Teichmann  
Black: O. Bernstein

`1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Nc3 Nf6 4. Bb5 d6 5. d4 Bd7 6. O-O Be7 7. Re1 exd4`

It has been well known since the game Tarrasch–Marco (Dresden, 1892) that castling for Black at the present moment entails the loss of a pawn after `8. Bxc6 Bxc6 9. dxe5 dxe5 10. Qxd8 Raxd8 11. Nxe5`, since `11... Bxe4 12. Nxe4 Nxe4` is impossible because of `13. Nd3`.

`8. Nxd4 O-O 9. Bxc6 bxc6`

This position can rightfully be called a theoretical opening position. It has occurred hundreds of times in practice, and even important authorities differ in their evaluations. Em. Lasker frequently and very successfully employed this variation for Black. However, many believed that White's advanced central e-pawn, a certain advantage in space and, finally, the weakness of Black's queenside pawns—which may play a role in the endgame—all make White's prospects more promising.

The diagrammed position is the starting point of planning for both opponents. White sets his sights on the advance e5. There have even been attempts to play it at once, but it became clear that after `10. e5 dxe5 11. Rxe5 Bd6` the position of Black's pieces improves, while White loses part of his achievement in the centre. Therefore the move e5 requires preparation. Black's task is to prevent the advance of the e-pawn by every possible means. To achieve this, he must create strong pressure on e5. At the same time, Black may make a demonstration on the open b-file, so as not to allow White to concentrate wholly on operations in the centre.

## CH2-3

`10. b3`

This move might be described as tactical and incidental, bearing the character of an oversight. Interestingly, the same “mistake” (perhaps too strong a word in the present case) had been made three years earlier in Forgacs–Wolf (Nuremberg, 1906).

The move `10. b3` is acceptable as a link in the plan to increase White's influence over e5, but `10. Bf4` serves this objective better. After White advances the pawn to b3, Black gains the possibility of the pawn march ...a5-a4 and, on this basis, a general offensive on the queen's flank. This offensive would appear quite logical after the bishop is transferred from e7 to g7, where it will restrain the advance e5 and at the same time support Black's attack on the a- and b-files from afar.

`10... Re8`

Black is carried away by his plan and does not notice the opportunity that has arisen to seize the initiative immediately. In the game mentioned above, Wolf played `10... d5!`, and after `11. e5 Bb4! 12. Bd2 Ng4` obtained the threats ...Qh4 and ...Bc5.

`11. Bb2 Bf8 12. Qd3 g6 13. Rad1 Bg7`

This position poses White a number of difficult problems. Black has created such a solid defensive line that it is very difficult for White to cross the frontier with e4-e5; without this, his heavy pieces, huddled together in the rear, are doomed to inactivity. He should therefore have persistently pursued his plan: the struggle for e5 with the aim of advancing the central pawn. The more consistent `14. f4` would have kept the initiative in White's hands.

Teichmann, however, is not one of those players inclined to take even a small risk, and cannot bring himself to do so. The move f4 appears to expose the king, permits the attacking reply ...Nh5, weakens e4 and, in the end, does not guarantee e4-e5. These considerations induce Teichmann to switch to defensive tactics, leading to further consolidation of the centre and the organisation of defence on the queen's flank.

`14. f3`

With this apparently modest move White neutralises the thrust ...Nh5, which, in conjunction with a future ...Be5 and ...Qh4, might begin a direct attack on the king's flank. For example, `14... Nh5 15. Bc1` (also possible is `15. Nde2 Qh4 16. Na4`) `15... Be5 16. Nde2 Qh4 17. f4! Nxf4 18. Bxf4 Bxf4 19. g3`, winning a piece.

Black therefore begins a plan of attack on the queen's flank.

## CH2-4

`14... Qb8 15. Bc1`

Retreat! Teichmann realises that his operation with `10. b3` has failed. In the general “orchestra” of the white pieces, the role of the bishop on b2 has proved worthless, and Teichmann transfers it to e3 so that it can take part in the coming events.

`15... Qb6 16. Na4`

On the immediate `16. Be3` there would have followed `16... c5 17. Nde2 a5`.

`16... Qb7 17. Nb2 c5 18. Ne2 Bb5`

This may be called an “intermediate move”. Before placing the bishop on the square assigned to it in the plan, c6, Black considers it useful to provoke c4. Why is this necessary? Black intends to organise an attack on the b3-pawn by ...a5-a4, and for this it is very important to deprive that pawn of the protection of the c2-pawn.

`19. c4 Bc6 20. Nc3`

White blockades a4 as far as possible and anticipates the advance of the a-pawn. A battle begins around this point, with Black as the attacking side after seizing the initiative on move 14.

`20... Nd7 21. Be3 Nb6 22. Rb1 a5 23. Bf2!`

A deeply planned move whose aim is not discovered by the opponent. As will be seen later, it in fact prevents the immediate advance of the black a-pawn. The black queen finds itself in an unpleasant opposition with the white rook; if Black intends to continue the attack on the queen's flank, it would be useful to remove the queen from the b-file to c8 or a6.

`23... a4?`

This move is a mistake. Admittedly, the pawn is recovered by force, which Bernstein had of course foreseen; but he fails to take into account that it is won back at the cost of the game. The conclusion is clear: an excessively direct execution of a plan can, despite a correct idea, lead to a deplorable result.

## CH2-5

`24. Nbxa4 Nxa4`

This too is unconsidered. Better was `24... Bxa4 25. bxa4 Qc6`; even then, however, the initiative passes to White after `26. Bh4 Nxa4 27. Nd5`.

`25. bxa4 Qa6 26. Re2`

Again an outwardly modest move, but in essence it prepares a powerful plan of attack on the king's flank. It is useful from several points of view: it prepares to double the rooks on the open b-file, frees e1 for the bishop and, in addition, defends the a2-pawn. The main, planned objective, however, comes to light only three moves later.

`26... Bxa4`

From move 23 onward Black goes downhill. He grabs the “poor” pawn without foreseeing the immediate punishment. A less pleasant but still lesser evil was `26... Bxc3`. The best defence, however, should be regarded as `26... Qc8`, followed by ...Qd7 or ...Qe6, placing the queen in the centre while retaining hopes of play on the a-file. In no circumstances can the white knight be allowed to reach d5 with tempo—that is, while creating a threat.

It is useful to note here that in chess, as in life, once one has gone astray it is not easy to retain self-control and, without losing one's presence of mind, begin searching for new paths under unfavourable conditions. We often see a player who, after making a mistake and losing the thread of his thought, becomes agitated, excited and irritated, begins to play badly and aggravates his difficulties. Black's play in this game after the mistaken advance ...a4 gives precisely such an impression of annoyance and irritation.

`27. Nd5!`

The beginning of a plan of attack which is brilliantly carried through to the end over the course of twenty moves and completed with a mating construction.

White now has the direct threat Nxc7, but the aim of the attack is not merely this. Its object is the weakened f6-square, for control of which the struggle now begins.

White's plan can be divided into three stages: first, the exchange of the bishop which is the principal defender of f6; second, the attack on f6 and the invasion of that square; third, the realisation of the invasion. All the elements of the general methods of planning—concrete ideas, dynamic play and harmony of forces—are vividly reflected in the course of executing the plan.

## CH2-6

`27... Ra7 28. Bh4`

This is why White played Bf2 on move 23. One move later, the planned basis of `26. Re2` is also revealed. Black cannot ignore the threat Nf6+ or Bf6 and begins defending the vulnerable f6-point in his castled position.

The continuation `28... Re6` clearly fails to `29. Rb8+`—Black opened the b-file, but White creates the threats on it. After `28... c6 29. Nf6+ Bxf6 30. Bxf6 Rd7 31. Qe3`, the threat Qh6 brings about Black's immediate downfall. Only one possibility remains.

`28... Bd4+ 29. Kh1 Kg7 30. Bf2!`

This forces the exchange of bishops, since `30... Be5` is met by `31. f4`, while after `30... Bf6 31. Nxf6 Kxf6 32. Bh4+` Black has a miserable position. The role of the white rook on e2 is now clear: it guarantees White the exchange of bishops.

`30... Bxf2 31. Rxf2`

Threatening a check from c3. The second stage of the plan begins: the struggle to invade with the knight on the weak f6-square. Realising that surrendering this square is “just like death”, Black makes heroic efforts to defend it, but after the exchange of bishops he has insufficient forces.

`31... Qa5 32. Qe2!`

Once again threatening a diagonal check, this time from b2.

`32... f6 33. Qb2 Rf8`

Black needs only one tempo to save himself. If it were now his turn, he would play ...c6, repulsing the attack on f6. But, alas, he does not have this tempo and the game is decided. White's threats, following one after another, do not give Black a moment's respite.

`34. g4!`

Threatening `35. Nxf6`. Black can postpone the fall of f6 for only three moves.

`34... h6 35. h4 g5 36. f4! gxh4 37. Nxf6`

And so the invasion takes place, after which the second stage of the plan may be considered complete. The third and concluding stage consists of realising the advantage achieved by the knight's invasion of f6. The position of the black king is utterly ruined, and his main forces are on the other flank, unable to arrive in time for the defence.

Meanwhile, White's threats are already impending. He now threatens `38. Nh5+ Kf7 39. Qf6+` and Rb8+.

## CH2-7

`37... Rf7 38. g5`

Now the g-file is opened and the rook enters the attack.

`38... Bc6 39. Rg1 Qa3 40. gxh6+ Kxh6 41. Rh2! Bxe4`

The sacrifice of a piece is the only defence against mate (if `41... Qf3+`, then `42. Qg2`). White's positional advantage grows into material gain. The agony does not last long.

`42. Nxe4 Qf3+ 43. Qg2 Qxg2+ 44. Rhxg2 Rxf4 45. Rg6+ Kh7 46. Nf6+ 1-0`

### Spanish Game

White: Y. Vilner  
Black: P. Romanovsky  
(3rd USSR Championship, 1924)

`1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bb5 d6 4. d4 Bd7 5. Nc3 Nge7`

Steinitz played this sometimes, although with this move Black delays the development of the king's flank by one tempo. Besides, the knight does not have as wide a range of activity from e7—particularly in the struggle for the centre—as it does from f6.

What, then, attracted Steinitz to this move? Its idea is to try to consolidate e5 after ...Ng6. After `5... Nf6 6. O-O Be7 7. Re1`, the e5-pawn is deprived of pawn support and Black has to exchange with ...exd4. For quite a long time, theoretical authorities, including Steinitz, regarded this exchange as a surrender of the centre. The move `5... Nge7` was one of Steinitz's numerous experiments.

`6. Bg5`

Chigorin played this against Steinitz in the eleventh game of their 1892 world championship return match. In several match games with Steinitz, Lasker continued `6. Bc4`, threatening Ng5. Vilner's opponent in the present game—the author of this book—intended to test Chigorin's recommendation and answer `6. Bc4` with `6... Na5`.

`6... h6`

Steinitz played `6... f6 7. Be3 Ng6` here. In the present game, without analysing concrete variations, Black feared that after `6... f6` the opponent would obtain the open a2-g8 diagonal, on which the bishop might be consolidated after `7. Be3 Ng6 8. a3!?`. However, exchanging the light-squared bishops with ...Nxd4 would create a weak square for Black on e6. Yet, after choosing an experimental system, Black had to think more boldly and concretely. The proverb “the sheep is safe when the wolf has had its fill” cannot be made the basis of a chess plan.

`7. Be3`

The apparently more logical `7. Bh4` surprisingly leads to difficulties in defending the d4-pawn after `7... g5 8. Bg3` (not `8. Nxg5 Ng6!`) `8... g4`. White would have to choose `9. Bxc6 Nxc6 10. d5 gxf3 11. dxc6 Bxc6 12. Qxf3`, when `12... Qg5` or ...h5 would give Black a good game.

It is interesting that Vilner feared not `7... g5`, but `7... Nxd4 8. Nxd4 exd4 9. Qxd4 Bxb5 10. Nxb5 Nc6! 11. Bxd8 Nxd4 12. Nxd4 Rxd8`. This too undoubtedly eases Black's position.

When planning a game, one often has to consider quite a few concrete continuations which justify or refute one idea or another.

`7... g6`

An incorrect scheme, inconsistent with the idea of developing the knight on e7. Black should strengthen e5 by ...Ng6 and complete development quickly with ...Be7 and ...O-O. The invasion of the white knight on d5 promises nothing concrete, and it could hardly hold its ground there.

`8. dxe5!`

The beginning of a plan that is very dangerous for Black. White has an advantage in development and intends to exploit it by opening the d-file. If Black replies ...dxe5, the king's bishop on g7 will be condemned to inactivity; after ...Nxe5, however, White is prepared to be satisfied with `9. Nxe5 dxe5` (`9... Bxb5? 10. Ng4`) `10. Bxd7+` (`10. Qe2` is also not bad) `10... Qxd7 11. Qxd7+ Kxd7 12. O-O-O+ Kc6 13. Rd3 Bg7 14. Rhd1`, with an indisputable advantage.

## CH2-8

`8... dxe5`

Black chooses the lesser of two evils and strives to create a complicated position in the difficult struggle ahead.

`9. Qd2`

White's plan is to control the d-file after castling queenside. At the same time, he wants to make it difficult for the opponent to castle. Nevertheless, the queen move does not take the immediate course of events into account with sufficient concreteness, and only one move later White has to transfer it to e2. It was better to do this at once, postponing the operation of the heavy pieces on the d-file until he had castled queenside.

`9... Bg7 10. O-O-O Bg4 11. Qe2`

After `11. Nd5`, Black has the adequate defence `11... a6`. White prefers to carry out his intended line, reconciling himself to the loss of a tempo.

`11... Qc8 12. Bc5 a6`

Black must defend against `13. Bxe7`, which can now be answered by `13... axb5`.

`13. Ba4`

White would also retain the better position after Bxc6+, but this would somewhat relax the tension. Black would probably have replied ...bxc6.

`13... b5 14. Bb3 O-O 15. Nd5!`

This move gives White the advantage and the chance to start an attack on the king's flank.

`15... Re8 16. h3 Be6 17. Qd2`

Played to prevent tactical possibilities connected with the opposition of the queen and black rook on e8. Such possibilities do arise after `17... Bxd5 18. exd5 Na5` or `18. Bxd5 Nxd5 19. exd5 Na5` (or ...Nd4).

There was, however, a more consistent way for White to occupy the d-file: `17. Nxe7+ Nxe7 18. Rd3`, followed by a build-up of the heavy pieces on the d-file.

`17... Rb8`

The aim of this move is to prepare an attack on b2. Black further intends ...a5, provoking a4, opening the b-file by exchanging on a4 and then trying to bring the bishop on g7 into play. All this is a rather hazy expectation, but it does cause White some concern.

`18. g4`

White is not afraid of ghosts. His attacking moves convey courage and belief in the correctness of his plan. He now threatens decisive progress by g5 followed by Nf6+.

`18... a5!`

Black sacrifices a pawn in the hope of enlivening his pieces somewhat and, principally, with the aim of distracting the opponent from executing his plan.

`19. g5`

Strangely, this attack is not so threatening. White should not refuse the gift with `19. Nxe7+ Nxe7 20. Qxa5`, although after `20... Bxb3 21. cxb3` (dangerous is `21. axb3 Nc6 22. Qc3 Ra8`, with initiative for the pawn) `21... Nc6` (worse is `21... Ra8 22. Qxb5 Rxa2 23. Ba3`) `22. Qc3 Qe6`, it would be very difficult to realise the extra pawn.

`19... Bxd5`

Of course, the check on f6 cannot be allowed.

`20. Bxd5`

It is hard to decide whether `20. exd5 a4 21. dxc6` was better. An interesting variation is `21. gxh6 axb3 22. hxg7 bxa2 23. Qh6 a1=Q+ 24. Kd2 Qxd1+ 25. Rxd1 f6`: White has no attack and is a rook down. After `21. dxc6 axb3 22. axb3 Nf5!`, Black gets promising play for the pawn.

`20... h5`

After this move, which locks the king's flank, White's attack comes to an end.

## CH2-9

The conditions of the struggle have changed radically. White has failed to carry out his plan; Black has rid himself of the troubles caused by his badly played opening. The opponents must look afresh at the position, consider new plans and spend time calculating possible variations.

The first question: can White, as a few moves ago, win a pawn by `21. Bxe7 Nxe7 22. Qxa5`? No. There follows `22... Nxd5 23. Rxd5` (even worse is `23. exd5 Qf5!`) `23... Ra8`; `24. Qxb5` is impossible because of `24... c6`, while after `24. Qe3`, Black simply takes the a2-pawn.

The second question: what should White do, and does the opponent threaten anything?

Black has no direct threats, but in general his forces are more actively placed than White's. The advance of Black's queenside pawn group may provide a motive for a plan aimed at the exposed position of the white king. Also possible is ...a4 and ...Nd8, threatening to win the bishop with ...c6. Finally, Black could improve the position of his pieces by ...Qa6 and ...Rbd8. White must also watch out for ...Nxd5: after exd5 the bishop on g7 comes back to life with ...e4, while after Qxd5 and ...Qb7 the advanced position of the white queen proves awkward.

All these considerations are clearly relative, since they do not bring out the full dynamics of the coming struggle, but they must nevertheless be taken into account. In view of this, White might contemplate only a very rough plan, whose guiding light must be the old but correct objective of occupying the d-file with the rooks.

`21. Qe3?`

In all probability, Vilner also regarded this move as a link in the plan to seize the d-file with the rooks. White intends Nh4 followed by Qf3. Sooner or later Black will have to take the bishop with ...Nxd5, after which Rxd5 and Rhd1 will achieve the objective. Incidentally, with the current arrangement of forces, the queen also defends h3.

Yet White heads toward his objective along a bad road. The queen finds itself in dangerous opposition to the enemy rook, and Black immediately exploits this fact. It follows that even an insufficiently well-conceived plan still has to be carried out skilfully: only one link of the chain need be “rusty” for the whole chain to fall apart.

White should have chosen `21. c3`, followed by Qc2 and doubling the rooks. With `21. c3` he would also have solved several subsidiary problems: eliminating Black's ideas connected with the knight thrust to d4; answering ...b4 with c4; and restricting the activity of the opponent's king's bishop by placing the pawn on c3.

`21... Nd4`

This sudden and unpleasant thrust has become possible because the white queen stands on the e-file.

`22. Bxe7`

White exchanges his best-placed piece for the knight, which has made only a single opening move—and not a particularly successful one at that. The decision was clearly dictated by the desire to preserve the light-squared bishop at any price. Yet this natural desire was again not confirmed by calculating variations, which would have shown White that the bishop could not be kept on the a2-g8 diagonal. In that case, is it worth preserving?

The correct continuation was `22. Nxd4 exd4 23. Qf4 Nxd5 24. exd5 Re5 25. Bxd4 Rf5! 26. Qg3 Bxd4 27. Rxd4 Qd8 28. Rg1 Rb6`, winning back one of the pawns and leading to an approximately equal position.

It should be noted that `22. Nxe5` was impossible because of `22... Nxd5`, when Black wins a piece (`23. exd5 Nf5` or `23. Qxd4 Nf4!`).

`22... Rxe7 23. c3`

Even worse is `23. a3 c6 24. Ba2 b4`, when Black obtains an attack.

`23... Nxf3 24. Qxf3 c6 25. Bb3 a4 26. Bc2 a3`

Black strives to create a weak square on d4 by eliminating the c3-pawn, and to exploit it as a base for the rooks and, if all goes well, also for the bishop.

`27. b3`

Tempting but weak was `27. Bb3`, which would be met by `27... c5 28. Bd5 axb2+ 29. Kxb2 c4!`; Black's attack on the a-file, supported by the bishop from f8, is rather dangerous. The white bishop may appear well placed on the weak central d5-square, but its role there is insignificant because it acts in isolation, without proper connection with the rest of White's forces.

`27... b4 28. c4`

White assumes that opposite-coloured bishops and the locked position will lead to a draw, and again contents himself with a general consideration. Yet concrete calculation would have convinced him of the weakness of the pawn advance.

In the course of the game, we have observed several times how White made not so much direct mistakes as mediocre moves, precisely because his choice was based on general principles. This applies to his 9th, 17th, 19th, 21st, 23rd and finally 28th move. Of course, there are positions where one must be guided by only very rough information about the value of one move or another, but in most cases matters are different. Assessing a position by calculating variations—that is, by analysis—will always be more accurate and will make it easier to find the right way to achieve the planned objective.

White's last move may be regarded as a fatal mistake. The best response to Black's energetic attack on the dark squares in his camp was `28. Bd3`, and if `28... bxc3`, then `29. Bc4`. Black would still retain the better position, but his activity would to some extent be tied to the necessary defence of f7. It should be added that the white bishop is now condemned to lifelong imprisonment behind its own pawns.

## CH2-10

`28... Re6`

The beginning of the execution of a plan consisting of four stages: occupation of d4 with the bishop and its consolidation there; preparation for opening the f-file; control of that file by the heavy pieces; invasion of the opponent's camp and organisation of a decisive attack on the king. Judging by White's reply, he apparently still does not discover this murderous intention.

`29. Rd3`

Is this really intended to double the rooks on the d-file? Alas, White does not suspect that three moves later his rooks will be in the same situation as his bishop.

`29... Bf8 30. Rhd1`

`30. Qe3` does not help, in view of `30... Be7`, followed by ...Qf8.

`30... Bc5 31. Qe2 Bd4`

Not fearing `32. f4`, because of `32... Bb2+` and ...exf4.

`32. Kb1`

White intends to withstand the siege. But this decision is incorrect, since Black has a real breakthrough plan at his disposal. It was better to sacrifice the exchange by `32. Rxd4 exd4 33. Rxd4`, although even then Black's task would not be very complicated: `33... Qc7! 34. Qd2 Ree8`, with the decisive threat ...Rbd8; if `35. Rd7`, then `35... Qe5`.

`32... c5`

And so the bishop is consolidated on the eternal d4-square. But it can be exploited as a real force only when harmony is established with the other black forces. For this it is necessary to organise an invasion by the rooks into the opponent's camp. This is obviously impossible along the open d-file. Another file must be opened—in the present case, the f-file.

However, the move ...f6 requires preparation. It opens not only the f-file but also the g-file, along which the white rooks could develop a dangerous counterattack against the g6-pawn and then, with the queen's support, against the black king. Black's attack on the f-file and White's counterattack on the g-file would take the form of a mighty battle between six heavy pieces, with the bishops inactive. For the present Black must, first, consolidate the position of his king in advance, particularly g6, and second, place his heavy pieces so that after the exchange of pawns he can strive for a breakthrough with all his might—that is, “build up” on the f-file. It is desirable to prepare this arrangement with the queen behind the rooks. Opening the f-file is plainly no simple operation, and the slightest carelessness might ruin the whole plan.

`33. Rf3`

White discovers his opponent's intention. However, both the rook move to f3 and all the subsequent manoeuvres of White's heavy pieces show that he is unable to construct a defensive plan. After the f-file is opened and occupied, Black threatens to win the f2-pawn, which is attacked four times but can be defended by only three white pieces. In the attack on f2, all the invasion squares—f1, f2 and f3—are open to the black rooks.

Therefore the relatively best defensive plan was to advance the pawn to f3, transfer the queen to g2 and free d1 for the bishop, from where it could cover the pawn one more time. This did not promise salvation, but it would at least have complicated Black's process of realisation.

`33... Qd8 34. h4 Qe7 35. Rg1 Rf8 36. Bd3 Kh7 37. Rfg3 Rd6 38. R1g2 f6`

The objective of the second stage is fulfilled: the f-file is opened and immediately occupied by Black's heavy pieces.

`39. gxf6`

If White continues to wait, for example with `39. Kc2`, there follows `39... Qf7`; `40. f3` is impossible because of `40... Rfd8`, with an unavoidable invasion of the black rooks, this time along the d-file.

`39... Rfxf6 40. Rg5 Qf7 41. Bc2 Rf4 42. Rh2 Rdf6`

This ends the third stage, and the struggle passes to its concluding phase: invasion and the swift “taking prisoner” of the king.

## CH2-11

`43. Rgg2 Bxf2`

At last the bishop enters into full contact with the rooks. This harmony signifies a quick end.

`44. Bd1 Bd4 45. Kc1 Rf1 46. Kc2 R6f3 47. Qd2 Rc3+ 48. Kb1 Qf3`

Black is carried away by the process of invasion. `48... Be3` would have provoked immediate capitulation. Even now, however, White is soon mated. The apotheosis of achieving the planned objective is illustrated by the diagram.

`49. Re2 Rxb3+ 50. axb3 Qxb3+ 51. Kc1 Bb2+ 0-1`

Mate follows on the next move.

In concluding this instructive game, we wish once again to recount the basic reasons for White's defeat. We have in mind not individual weak moves or direct oversights, but the very essence of the errors in chess thinking. From this point of view, the basic reason must be seen in a non-concrete approach to many positions arising in the course of the struggle. There were too few variations in White's thinking when evaluating positions, and this prevented him from foreseeing even approximately the immediate course of events on the board.

Thus White's 28th move, c4, testifies that he did not foresee the manoeuvre ...Bg7-f8-c5-d4 at all. When the bishop invaded d4, he was unable to weigh the associated dangers correctly and, instead of immediately sacrificing the exchange, allowed the opponent to play ...c5. Had White imagined the consequences of the ...f6 break, he would of course not have permitted the construction of a real stronghold on d4. The non-concrete approach to the position was essentially a breach of the principles of dynamic play. White's thinking was undynamic: he assessed the position statically, not in the light of the continuous development of the struggle.

Besides these main reasons, White's mistake must also be regarded as the loss, to some extent, of steadfastness in improving his position. If one yields to a feeling of inevitable defeat, one's thinking begins almost to run idle. A weakened will to resist shows itself in indifference to the creative side of the game. In the present game, White's play after `21... Nd4` gives the impression of demoralisation, and after `29... Bf8`, even of total doom.

## CH2-12

### Philidor Defence

White: A. Evenson  
Black: A. Alekhine  
(Kiev, 1916)

`1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 d6 3. d4 Nf6 4. Nc3 Nbd7`

One of the principal variations of the Philidor Defence has been played. However, the knight on d7 hampers Black and makes development of his queen's flank difficult. Therefore another plan of development is now put forward: `4... exd4 5. Nxd4 Be7 6. Be2 O-O 7. O-O a6 8. a4 c5 9. Nf3 Nc6`. Unfortunately there is still too little practical experience to permit conclusions, but in any case it has no less right to exist than the stereotyped theoretical variation chosen by Black in the present game. In the latter, Black usually has to overcome certain difficulties before emerging safely into the broad scope of the middlegame.

`5. Bc4 Be7 6. O-O O-O 7. dxe5`

White has no reason to fear the exchange ...exd4, which would only strengthen his position in the centre. Yet Evenson's decision to open the d-file is easy to understand, since `7. Qe2`, recommended in practice and approved in opening handbooks, justifies itself only after the book reply `7... c6`.

However, precisely after `7. Qe2`, Black has an interesting possibility with an exchange on d4: for example, `7... exd4 8. Nxd4 Ne5 9. Bb3 c5 10. Nf5 Bxf5 11. exf5 Nc6`, or ...Qd7, with excellent prospects.

The move `7. h3` creates the greatest difficulties for Black. In the variation just mentioned, after `8... Ne5`, it allows `9. Be2`, threatening to begin an offensive with f4. After `9... d5`, the simple `10. exd5 Nxd5 11. Nxd5 Qxd5 12. Nf5!` is possible, and White's position is preferable. Besides, `7. h3` prevents the knight thrust to g4 and thereby secures e3 for the bishop.

After Be3, White could move the knight from f3 and attack e5 once again with f4. A rough course of events might be `7. h3 c6 8. a4 Qc7` (`8... Nxe4 9. Nxe4 d5 10. Bxd5 cxd5 11. Nc3` would create a lasting weakness for Black on d5) `9. Be3`, when Black is cramped, while White threatens Nh2 or Nd2 followed by f4.

After Evenson's exchange in the centre, the rooks will be drawn to the only open d-file, and if neither opponent succeeds in outstripping the other in this operation, one must expect a mass exchange—the herald of a drawing tendency.

## CH2-13

`7... dxe5`

White's plan is now obvious: clear the way for the rooks to the d-file and try to double them there. The route to this objective is also clear. It consists of Qe2, Be3 or Bg5, Rfd1, a4 if necessary to prevent ...b5, and possibly h3 so as not to allow ...Ng4 after Be3 and to prevent the pin ...Bg4. It is necessary, however, to decide in what order these moves should be made.

To answer this question, one must probably state concretely what plan Black should choose to bring the rooks to d8 and neutralise the opponent's play on the d-file. In order to develop his queen's flank, Black has to free the queen's knight from the defence of e5.

The most convenient method is to defend e5 once more with the queen from c7; incidentally, ...Qc7 also frees d8 for the rooks. Thus: `8. Qe2 c6 9. a4 Qc7 10. h3 Nc5 11. Rfd1 a5 12. Bg5 Be6 13. Rd2 Rad8`. It is not hard to convince oneself that White does not succeed in controlling the d-file. Consequently, the exchange `7. dxe5` does not achieve its objective and is a link in a mistaken plan.

`8. Bg5 c6 9. a4 Qc7 10. Qe2 Nc5 11. Ne1`

Having become convinced that the rooks' entrance onto the d-file leads only to exchanges, White apparently does not wish to take the road to a prospective draw—though he himself created it with move seven—and changes his plan. He is prepared to concede the d-file, counting on preventing the invasion of the black rooks with a knight covering d3, and intends to prepare his own attack on the centre with f4 and the subsequent opening of the f-file.

`11... Ne6 12. Be3 Nd4 13. Qd1`

`13. Bxd4 exd4` is less attractive, and the second knight would have to retreat to the first rank.

`13... Rd8`

Black accepts the gift of the open d-file. His plan will consist of three stages: doubling the rooks on this file and removing the knight from d3; invasion of the opponent's territory; realisation of the advantage achieved.

He will need more than thirty moves to fulfil the plan: the first part takes ten moves, the second six and the third twenty. Thus, the process of realisation can be very protracted and complicated. In the previous game it took only a few moves, since the opponent was routed during the invasion itself; here, however, as we shall see, White retains considerable resources for resistance even after the rooks invade.

`14. Nd3 Be6 15. Bxe6 Nxe6 16. Qe1 Rd7 17. f3`

This advance testifies that White has finally decided to switch to defence; now Black realises his plan without hindrance. Clearly it was necessary to continue consistently with `17. f4`, to which Alekhine intended `17... Ng4`, and if `18. f5`, then `18... Rxd3 19. cxd3 Nxe3`; `20. Qxe3` is impossible because of `20... Bc5`. But instead of `20. Qxe3`, White could take the other knight with `20. fxe6`, followed by `20... Nxf1 21. exf7+ Kxf7 22. Qxf1+ Kg8`. Admittedly, the resulting position is still more favourable for Black. The threats ...Bc5+ or ...Qb6+ followed by ...Rf8 are rather unpleasant. All the same, White could defend with `23. Qe2`.

After `17. f3`, however, Black slowly but surely takes control of the whole board.

## CH2-14

`17... Rad8 18. Bf2 Nh5`

The beginning of the “hunt” for the knight on d3.

`19. Ne2 c5 20. b3 Nhf4 21. Nexf4 Nxf4 22. Nxf4 exf4`

This completes the first stage of the plan and begins the second: the invasion along the open file. The invasion square—a weak square on an open line—is d2. True, it can be covered by `23. Qc1 Rd2 24. Be1`, but Black's main threat is not the direct ...Rd2; it is the preliminary ...c4-c3.

`23. c3`

This forestalls the threat just mentioned and, after `23... c4 24. b4`, covers c5, where the black bishop might otherwise take up a very powerful position. In return, however, White creates another weak square on the d-file: d3.

`23... Qe5`

Black is guaranteed an invasion on d3; for the present he improves the position of his queen and bishop.

`24. Ra2 Rd3 25. Rc2 b6`

Threatening to win a pawn by ...Bf6.

`26. Qc1 Qe6 27. Qb1 Bf6 28. b4 c4 29. Qc1 g5`

Black begins to execute the concluding part of the plan. All his pieces occupy ideal positions. Admittedly, the invading rook itself cannot deliver the decisive blow, but by paralysing White's forces it assists in a series of operations leading to a further deterioration of his position. The pawn advance on the king's flank undertaken by Alekhine is probably the most energetic way for Black to realise his advantage, personified by the menacing and unassailable position of the rook on the weak d3-square.

## CH2-15

`30. h3 Be5 31. Qa1 h5 32. a5`

White opens the a-file, but the opponent's attack develops so rapidly that he will not have time to exploit this airway. Had he wished, Black could have deprived White even of this possibility by playing ...a6.

`32... g4 33. axb6 axb6 34. Bh4 f6 35. Be1 g3`

Finally suffocating the opponent. The final objective is the queen's invasion along the g1-a7 diagonal to e3.

`36. Qa6 Qc6`

Threatening ...Ra8.

`37. Qa3`

The continuation `37. Ra2 Bxc3` was also hopeless, as indeed was everything else. White reaps the fruits of his two mistaken plans, begun by `11. Ne1` and `17. f3`.

The consequences of bad moves are not always ruinous, but following an incorrect plan is indeed “like death”.

`37... b5 38. Qb2 Qb6+ 39. Kh1 Rd1`

Not at once `39... Qe3`, in view of `40. Re2`; but now Black threatens `40... Qe3 41. Re2 Qxc3`. Neither `40. Rg1 Qxg1+` nor `40. Re2 R8d3`, threatening ...Bxc3, offers any help.

There followed:

`40. Rc1 Qe3 41. Ra1 Bc7 42. Qa2 Rxa1 43. Qxa1 Qe2 44. Rg1 Bb6`

and four moves later, `0-1`.
